# Research Vision

## The object of inquiry

Enterprise software is normally approached as a technology selected, implemented, and used by organisations. That orientation has produced important knowledge about implementation, adaptation, resistance, organisational change, and value realisation. It nevertheless places the organisation analytically before the technology: an organisation has structures and routines, introduces a system, and then experiences consequences. This programme reverses the direction of inquiry without denying the established one. It asks what model of organisation can be reconstructed from the system's reference architecture itself.

An enterprise system is not merely a neutral container for transactions. To make transactions computable, its designers must specify actors, objects, permissible actions, sequences, states, dependencies, visibility, decisions, exceptions, and completion conditions. A purchase request becomes actionable because the architecture identifies who may create it, which attributes it must contain, when it becomes an approval task, who can decide, what a rejection means, and which later activities depend upon its status. Such constructs can be read technically, as software functionality, and organisationally, as an explicit division and coordination of work. The programme calls the latter *encoded organisational logic*.

The claim is not that code determines organisation. Reference architectures are selected, configured, extended, integrated, circumvented, and enacted in situated practice. A documentary analysis cannot establish how any adopting organisation actually works. The analytically necessary distinction is therefore between (1) encoded organisational logic, the arrangements represented and made available in a reference architecture; (2) implemented organisational configuration, the selected and configured system in a particular organisation; and (3) enacted organisational practice, the situated accomplishment of work around and through that configuration. Paper 1 is deliberately bounded to the first category.

## Coordination as the central problem

Organisation design begins with differentiation and dependency. Work is divided among people and units, yet the resulting activities must be brought into a coherent relation. Thompson's distinction among pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence supplies a disciplined vocabulary for identifying different dependency structures. Mintzberg directs attention to the mechanisms by which differentiated work is coordinated: direct supervision, standardisation of work, outputs, and skills, and mutual adjustment. Galbraith adds that uncertainty creates information-processing requirements and that design choices allocate information capacity.

Enterprise software makes these issues empirically visible. Process sequences render some dependencies explicit; statuses and shared objects connect activities; workflows route work; validations impose common premises; queues collect tasks; and exception signals redirect attention. The research does not assume a one-to-one correspondence between a software feature and a theoretical mechanism. A workflow may standardise work, allocate decision authority, and transmit information simultaneously. Conversely, reciprocal interdependence may be incompletely represented in a process model. The empirical task is to reconstruct configurations of mechanisms and assess where classical categories illuminate, overlap, or fail.

Coordination matters because the system's architecture defines more than efficiency. It can establish whose action is prerequisite to another's, which information becomes common, whether an exception stops a process or merely signals it, and whether adjustment is possible inside the workflow. These arrangements are organisation-design choices in encoded form. Their effects remain contingent upon implementation and enactment, but their presence and internal logic can be studied directly.

## Governance, decision rights, and control

Coordination alone does not explain who is authorised to decide or how conduct is rendered accountable. Governance concerns the allocation and constraint of decision rights, access rights, approval authority, and responsibility. Control concerns the mechanisms that compare action or output with a rule, threshold, plan, or expected state and produce a consequence. Enterprise architectures instantiate these concerns through role catalogues, organisational restrictions, approval conditions, segregation-of-duties arrangements, document blocks, validations, audit-relevant status changes, and monitoring views.

The architecture may separate initiating, approving, executing, and recording activities. It may restrict a role to a company code or purchasing organisation. It may permit a business expert to configure approval thresholds but not alter the underlying workflow scenario. These are not simply interface characteristics: they delimit domains of legitimate organisational action. Yet interpretation must be cautious. A role template is an offered allocation, not proof of a job design; an authorisation possibility is not proof of assignment; and a technical log does not by itself establish social accountability.

Governance is also inseparable from discretion. Public-cloud software typically combines fixed application logic, predefined content, selectable scope, configurable parameters, extension points, and exceptional interventions. Rather than opposing standardisation and flexibility, the research examines how discretion is bounded, located, and made governable. Who may configure which condition, at what stage, using which sanctioned mechanism? This shifts analysis from whether a package is flexible to the organisational distribution of flexibility.

## Information processing as an organisational architecture

Galbraith's information-processing perspective makes shared data and workflow signals analytically central. Organisational units confront uncertainty when they lack the information required to perform or coordinate tasks. Enterprise systems respond by defining data objects, ownership conditions, integration points, visibility restrictions, aggregations, and exception messages. A master-data object can standardise categories across transactions; a status can compress a history into an actionable signal; an analytical view can aggregate dispersed events; and a workflow item can transfer both information and an obligation to act.

Information integration should not be treated as an undifferentiated virtue. A common database or data model does not imply universal visibility, shared interpretation, or good information. Access restrictions deliberately partition visibility. Required fields can improve comparability while suppressing contextual nuance. Aggregation can support oversight while distancing decision-makers from local conditions. The study will therefore code distinct information operations—creation, transfer, aggregation, integration, visibility, exception signalling, and uncertainty reduction—and relate them to specific dependencies and governance arrangements.

## Why SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition

SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition is theoretically useful because it offers a comparatively explicit reference architecture spanning enterprise structure, end-to-end processes, business roles, authorisations, workflows, master data, configuration, and extensibility. SAP Signavio Process Navigator, SAP Help Portal, SAP Best Practices content, and the Fiori Apps Reference Library expose multiple views of the architecture. The public-cloud model is especially revealing because standard content, upgrade discipline, key-user configuration, and released extension mechanisms make the boundary between vendor template and customer discretion more visible than in an unconstrained bespoke system.

The case is instrumental rather than representative by assertion. SAP's market importance and architectural breadth make it a strong setting in which to develop the method and concepts. They do not establish that findings generalise to other ERP platforms. The initial comparison across P2P, O2C, and R2R provides theoretical variation within the case: material and supplier dependencies, customer fulfilment and credit relations, and periodic financial integration pose different coordination and control problems.

Official documentation is itself an interested representation. It describes intended capabilities, may privilege normative paths, and changes with releases. That is not a reason to discard it; reference architecture is precisely the object. It does require source triangulation, release control, negative-case analysis, and separation between what a process diagram depicts, what product documentation permits, and what the researcher infers organisationally.

## Software organisational primitives

The programme develops the tentative concept of a *software organisational primitive*: a recurring software construct through which an organisational coordination, governance, information-processing, or control mechanism is instantiated. Candidates include roles, workflows, organisational units, rules, statuses, approvals, blocks, access rights, master-data objects, handoffs, and system-generated events.

The concept earns its place only if it improves explanation. A primitive must be identifiable in artefacts, recur across instances, have a specifiable computational or representational operation, and carry a plausible organisational mechanism. Purely cosmetic interface elements, implementation labels without operative consequences, and broad modules are excluded. The same primitive may participate in different mechanisms depending on its relations. A status is organisationally consequential when it gates subsequent work, changes visibility, signals an exception, or anchors accountability; a decorative label is not.

Analysis proceeds at three levels. Micro-analysis identifies primitives and local mechanisms. Meso-analysis reconstructs how primitives form a process-level coordination and governance configuration. Macro-analysis compares processes and structural components to infer the broader encoded model of organisation. Macro claims require recurrence across independent artefacts and explicit treatment of counter-evidence.

## Fit-to-Standard as a sensitising concept

Fit-to-Standard can be interpreted provisionally as a negotiation between a vendor's encoded organisational template and an adopting organisation's existing coordination arrangements. Official guidance describes workshops that compare required processes with standard content and identify gaps. Organisation theory suggests a more precise question: which dependencies, decision rights, information needs, and controls are accepted, reconfigured, extended, or left outside the system?

Paper 1 cannot establish negotiation dynamics from architecture documents alone. Fit-to-Standard therefore remains a sensitising concept and a bridge to a later study using workshop observations, decision logs, configuration records, and interviews. Treating it prematurely as a finding would collapse encoded logic into implementation practice.

## Technical and organisational architecture

Technical architecture specifies components, services, data models, interfaces, and execution dependencies. Organisational architecture specifies differentiation, authority, information relations, coordination, and control. The two intersect without becoming identical. A service call can be technically important yet organisationally uninformative; a role restriction can be technically simple yet organisationally consequential. The method reconstructs organisational architecture from technical and process artefacts by making each inferential step inspectable and by recording alternative technical explanations.

This distinction prevents metaphor from replacing analysis. The study will not label every technical dependency an organisational interdependence. It asks whether the dependency links differentiated organisational activities or responsibilities and whether the architecture supplies a coordination mechanism. Similarly, a database constraint counts as formal organisational control only where it governs an organisationally meaningful action or object.

## Longer-term programme

The first paper establishes an object, vocabulary, and reproducible analytical procedure. Subsequent studies can examine Fit-to-Standard as design negotiation, configuration as bounded discretion, variation across ERP vendors, and differences between encoded and enacted arrangements. Comparative work should test whether apparently universal primitives conceal vendor-specific organisational logics.

AI agents sharpen the programme. When enterprise software moves from routing human tasks to recommending, initiating, or completing actions, software components may occupy roles formerly understood as organisational actors. Questions of delegation, escalation, observability, contestability, and decision rights become central. The present framework offers a baseline: before analysing agentic coordination, researchers need to understand how conventional enterprise architectures already encode action and authority.

The long-term intellectual concern is socio-technical coordination across complex organisational networks. Enterprise software links firms, suppliers, customers, platforms, regulators, and automated actors. Studying how architectures define value-bearing objects, visibility, authority, and dependencies can connect organisation design to digital infrastructure and network governance. Paper 1 remains narrower: it asks what can be learned, rigorously and without deterministic claims, by reading a reference architecture as encoded organisation design.
