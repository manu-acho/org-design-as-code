# Stream Six: Technology, Materiality, and Organisation

## Scope and evidence boundary

This review draws on independently verified scholarly publications. Claims based only on abstracts are limited to what those abstracts establish, and exact published versions must be consulted before page citation. Literature findings, project interpretations, and propositions for later research are distinguished throughout.

Technical arrangements can support conditional statements about possible action. They do not establish what actors perceive, which affordances they actualise, how situated practice develops, or what organisational outcomes follow.

The central question is not whether technology determines organisation or whether people freely determine technology. It is how technical arrangements become consequential in relation with actors, goals, knowledge, practices, and institutional conditions. That question matters directly to this project because vendor documentation can show material and symbolic arrangements represented in enterprise software, but it cannot by itself show how those arrangements are perceived, used, worked around, or made consequential in a particular organisation.

## Part I: Learning guide

### 1. The practical problem

Enterprise software is easy to describe in functional language. A workflow routes an item. A permission restricts an action. A validation checks a field. A shared record makes information available. An automation performs a transition.

Each statement may be technically accurate, but none is yet a complete organisational explanation. A shared record may be present without being trusted. A permission may exist without conferring accepted authority. A workflow may route an item while people coordinate the difficult part through calls or messages. A validation may prevent one error while encouraging work outside the system. An automation may execute an action without deciding the organisational purpose for which it is used.

Stream Six supplies the conceptual discipline needed to move from a technical feature to a bounded organisational claim:

> Technical properties condition possibilities for action, but organisational consequences depend on relational, situated, and temporally unfolding processes of perception, use, adaptation, and actualisation.

This formulation rejects two shortcuts. The first treats technology as an independent force with predictable effects. The second treats material properties as infinitely open to interpretation. Material arrangements matter, but they matter in relation with particular actors and practices.

### 2. Seven concepts to master

#### Duality of technology

**Definition:** Orlikowski (1992) uses a structurational model to explain technology as both shaped through human action and implicated in the conditions of later action.

**Plain-language explanation:** People design, implement, interpret, and use technologies. Once present, those technologies also enter the circumstances within which later work occurs.

**Research use:** The concept prevents the project from treating SAP architecture as either an autonomous organisational cause or a neutral container.

**Evidence boundary:** Documentary architecture can establish represented technical arrangements. It cannot establish how a customer organisation produced, interpreted, or reproduced those arrangements in practice.

#### Technology-in-practice

**Definition:** Orlikowski (2000) shifts attention from a technology as a fixed package of structures to structures enacted through recurrent interaction with technology.

**Plain-language explanation:** The same technical system can become part of different ways of working because people draw on it differently in ongoing practice.

**Research use:** Technology-in-practice marks the boundary between a system's documented capabilities and its situated organisational use.

**Evidence boundary:** A documented feature is not a technology-in-practice. Establishing the latter requires evidence of recurrent situated use.

#### Materiality

**Definition:** Materiality concerns those physical or digital properties and arrangements through which technologies make some actions possible, difficult, visible, durable, or unavailable.

**Plain-language explanation:** Software is not immaterial simply because it is digital. Data structures, interfaces, rules, required fields, integrations, and processing logic can make practical differences.

**Research use:** Materiality directs attention to what the enterprise system is arranged to do and not merely to what users say about it.

**Evidence boundary:** Material properties do not carry one universal organisational meaning or effect. Their consequences must be studied in relation with actors, purposes, and conditions.

#### Sociomateriality

**Definition:** Orlikowski (2007) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008) argue that the social and material are constitutively entangled in organisational practice rather than independent elements that only later interact.

**Plain-language explanation:** In actual work, people, categories, devices, records, algorithms, spaces, and routines may be so intertwined that separating a purely social process from a purely technical one distorts the phenomenon.

**Research use:** Sociomateriality is especially valuable for later research on enacted fit, where the object of inquiry is practice rather than a standalone system or user.

**Evidence boundary:** Jones (2014), Mutch (2013), and Leonardi (2013) show that sociomaterial approaches differ in ontology and empirical tractability. The term should not be used as a general synonym for any study involving people and technology.

#### Affordance

**Definition:** In organisational information-systems research, an affordance is a relational possibility for action arising between an actor or organised group and a technical object or arrangement (Hutchby, 2001; Markus and Silver, 2008; Volkoff and Strong, 2013).

**Plain-language explanation:** An affordance is not simply a feature and not simply a user intention. It is something a particular actor could do with a particular arrangement under particular conditions.

**Research use:** Affordances help the project discuss possible organisational action without claiming that the action occurs.

**Evidence boundary:** Documentary evidence may support a carefully bounded inference about an apparent or candidate affordance. It does not establish that relevant actors perceive or actualise it.

#### Constraint

**Definition:** A constraint is a relational condition through which material arrangements limit or make action more difficult in relation to an actor's goal (Leonardi, 2011).

**Plain-language explanation:** A property may constrain one activity while enabling another. A mandatory validation can obstruct rapid exception handling while supporting data completeness.

**Research use:** The concept helps analyse required fields, fixed sequences, access restrictions, thresholds, and blocked transitions without assuming their organisational effects.

**Evidence boundary:** A technical prohibition can often be established from design evidence. Wider constraints, such as delay, loss of discretion, or work intensification, require situated empirical evidence.

#### Affordance actualisation

**Definition:** Actualisation is the process through which actors take action to realise a technological possibility in an organisational setting (Volkoff and Strong, 2013; Strong et al., 2014).

**Plain-language explanation:** A possibility does not become an outcome automatically. People must recognise, organise around, and repeatedly act through it, often across levels and over time.

**Research use:** Actualisation provides the missing process between represented technical potential and realised organisational consequence.

**Evidence boundary:** Actualisation cannot be inferred from feature availability or configuration alone. It requires evidence of actors, actions, conditions, and effects over time.

### 3. The six-layer analytical chain

The following chain is a project synthesis. It is informed by Streams Four to Six, but it is not presented as one established model from a single source.

| Layer | Question | Suitable evidence | Prohibited shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Represented architecture | What roles, objects, rules, sequences, permissions, and technical relations does the reference material describe? | Vendor documentation, process models, schemas, and technical specifications | Calling the representation organisational practice |
| Configured technology | Which options, rules, data structures, integrations, and authorisations were selected in an implementation? | Configuration records, design decisions, system inspection, and test evidence | Assuming configuration is used as intended |
| Implemented arrangement | What technical and organisational arrangement was put into operation? | Deployment records, operating procedures, training, roles, and implementation evidence | Treating go-live as successful enactment |
| Perceived possibility or constraint | What do particular actors believe the arrangement allows or restricts relative to their goals? | Interviews, observation, elicitation, and situated accounts | Reading perception directly from a feature |
| Enacted practice | How do actors recurrently work with, around, or through the arrangement? | Observation, repeated event evidence, communication records, and longitudinal study | Treating a system event or stated procedure as the complete practice |
| Realised outcome | What consequences emerge, for whom, at what level, and over what period? | Comparative and longitudinal outcome evidence linked to mechanisms | Attributing an outcome to technology availability alone |

The layers are connected without being collapsible. A study may legitimately stop at represented architecture, provided its claims stop there too.

### 4. A short reading route

1. Read Orlikowski (1992) for the duality of technology and the rejection of simple objectivist and voluntarist accounts.
2. Read Orlikowski (2000) for technology-in-practice and situated enactment.
3. Read Hutchby (2001), Markus and Silver (2008), and Leonardi (2011) for material possibilities, functional affordances, constraints, and imbrication.
4. Read Orlikowski (2007) and Orlikowski and Scott (2008) for constitutive entanglement and the sociomaterial programme.
5. Read Jones (2014), Mutch (2013), and Leonardi (2013) to understand why sociomateriality is not one uncontested position.
6. Read Volkoff and Strong (2013) and Strong et al. (2014) for affordance actualisation and organisational change.
7. Return to Strong and Volkoff (2010) and Stream Four to connect material arrangements with enterprise-system fit and misfit.

### Study pause: trace one feature without skipping a layer

Take an approval workflow and ask:

1. What does the reference architecture represent?
2. What was configured locally?
3. What was actually implemented, including roles and supporting procedures?
4. Which actors perceive the workflow as enabling or constraining, and relative to which goals?
5. How is approval work enacted in routine and exceptional cases?
6. Which outcomes are observed, for whom, and against what comparison?

If a layer is unsupported, mark it as unknown. Do not fill it with a plausible story.

## Part II: Readable scholarly synthesis

### 5. From technological impact to the duality of technology

Early research on technology and organisation often moved between two unsatisfactory positions. One treated technology as an external force producing organisational effects. The other emphasised interpretation and social choice so strongly that the properties of the artefact became difficult to see.

Orlikowski (1992) responds by adapting structuration theory. Technology is a product of human action and a medium through which later action occurs. Designers and users act within institutional conditions. Their actions create, stabilise, modify, and use technical arrangements. Those arrangements then enter later practice without mechanically determining it.

The contribution is not a compromise in which social and technical causes are simply added together. It is a temporal and recursive explanation. What has been designed and institutionalised becomes part of the conditions of later action, while those conditions may be reinforced or changed through use.

For enterprise software, this means that standard architecture has organisational significance before any claim about outcomes is made. It embodies categories, sequences, data relations, and action possibilities produced through prior design. Yet its existence does not determine how one customer organisation will configure or enact it.

### 6. The practice lens relocates structure into recurrent use

Orlikowski (2000) develops a practice lens around technologies-in-practice. People enact structures when they recurrently interact with technologies. Their use draws on facilities, norms, knowledge, power, and prior experience. The resulting practice may reproduce familiar arrangements or generate change.

This move explains why feature lists are insufficient for organisational analysis. Two groups may have access to the same system and enact different technologies-in-practice. One may use a shared record as the authoritative basis for coordination. Another may enter the minimum required data while relying on a spreadsheet and informal conversation.

The practice lens does not imply that any use is possible. Material properties and institutional conditions remain relevant. It does imply that the organisational structure associated with use cannot be read directly from design.

This is the decisive boundary for Paper One. The paper can reconstruct an encoded organisation from reference artefacts. It cannot claim to observe a technology-in-practice because no situated customer use is being studied.

### 7. Materiality prevents social explanation from making the artefact disappear

Leonardi and Barley (2008) argue that materiality has remained under-theorised partly because scholars conflate materialism with determinism. Recognising that artefacts have properties does not require believing that those properties produce fixed effects.

Hutchby (2001) makes a related intervention through affordances. Technologies are neither blank texts nor autonomous forces. Their materiality offers some possibilities and closes others. The relation is practical: a possibility exists for an actor in relation to an artefact, not as a free-floating feature.

Markus and Silver (2008) bring this reasoning into information-systems research by distinguishing technical objects, functional affordances, and symbolic expressions. A technical object is not identical to what an actor can do with it, and neither is identical to the meanings it expresses.

This distinction is useful for enterprise software. A role-based permission is a technical arrangement. The possibility of authorising a transaction is a relational action possibility. The status, legitimacy, or symbolism attached to that permission belongs to another analytical question. Streams Three and Eight are needed before permission can be equated with authority or accountability.

### 8. Affordance and constraint are relational, not labels for features

Leonardi (2011) studies how people interpret technologies as affording or constraining action in relation to their goals. He develops imbrication as a way to explain how human and material agencies become interlocked over time in routines and technologies.

The idea is often simplified into a list of technological affordances. That loses the relational core. Searchability, visibility, editability, or automation may describe properties or broad capacities. Whether a particular arrangement affords coordinated action depends on who is acting, what they are trying to achieve, what they know, what authority they hold, and what other arrangements are present.

Constraints are equally relational. A required field may constrain a hurried employee seeking to complete a transaction, while affording a later controller the possibility of consistent checking. The same arrangement can therefore participate in different and even conflicting organisational consequences.

For this project, a documentary analysis may identify a candidate affordance by connecting a represented arrangement to a specified class of actor and action. The wording must remain conditional. The analysis cannot establish perception, use, or effect without organisational evidence.

### 9. Sociomateriality changes the unit of analysis

Orlikowski (2007) argues for studying sociomaterial practices in which the social and material are constitutively entangled. Orlikowski and Scott (2008) show how much organisation research had treated technology as absent or peripheral, then develop sociomateriality as a distinct research direction.

The practical consequence is a change in the unit of analysis. Rather than explaining a social organisation interacting with an external technology, the researcher may study a phenomenon that is already performed through people, devices, data, categories, spaces, and algorithms.

This approach is powerful for enacted work. It can reveal why an online valuation, a clinical judgement, or a financial close is not a purely human activity later supported by a tool. The phenomenon is materially organised from the outset.

It is less straightforward for the present documentary phase. Reference architecture can reveal technical and representational relations, but constitutive entanglement is a claim about practice. Paper One should therefore use sociomateriality primarily as a boundary discipline and a design for later empirical inquiry, not as evidence that a customer phenomenon has already been observed.

### 10. The field contains real theoretical disagreement

Sociomateriality should not be presented as a settled vocabulary. Jones (2014) finds that studies use its constituent ideas selectively and argues that greater attention is needed to differences among materiality, inseparability, relationality, performativity, and practice.

Mutch (2013) criticises accounts that, in his view, insufficiently specify technology and neglect wider social structures. He proposes critical realism as an alternative foundation. Leonardi (2013) similarly compares agential realism and critical realism and draws attention to the difficulty of mapping philosophical commitments onto empirical phenomena.

The disagreement matters methodologically. A strong entanglement position may resist separating social and material elements even for analysis. A critical-realist affordance account treats actors and artefacts as distinct but related and seeks mechanisms through which their relation produces effects. An imbrication account preserves different forms of agency while explaining how they become interlocked over time.

The project need not force these positions into one model. It should use each for the question it can answer, state its ontological commitment, and avoid combining incompatible terms without explanation.

### 11. Actualisation explains why potential is not outcome

Volkoff and Strong (2013) theorise affordances as generative mechanisms arising from relations between organisational and technical arrangements. Actors may actualise these affordances over time, producing observable effects under particular conditions.

Strong et al. (2014) develop a mid-range theory from a longitudinal electronic-health-record implementation. Their account addresses organisational affordances, bundles of interrelated affordances, and the process through which actors actualise them. Its substantive findings are bounded to the healthcare case, but its process logic is valuable beyond that setting.

Actualisation clarifies four distinctions that enterprise-software research often compresses:

1. a feature or arrangement can exist;
2. a relational possibility for action can arise;
3. actors can take action to realise that possibility; and
4. consequences can emerge, including consequences different from those intended.

This sequence is not automatic and may be recursive. Outcomes from one actualisation may alter later goals, resources, interpretations, and possibilities.

### 12. The bridge to enterprise-system fit

Strong and Volkoff (2010) show that enterprise-system fit is multidimensional. Their study identifies misfits involving functionality, data, usability, roles, control, and organisational culture, and distinguishes deficiencies from impositions.

Stream Four uses this evidence to analyse implementation fit. Stream Six adds the mechanism question. A configured arrangement may cover a requirement yet fail to afford effective action for relevant actors. An apparent imposition may be accommodated, resisted, bypassed, or turned into a new practice. These are empirical possibilities, not conclusions that can be drawn from architecture alone.

The combined lesson is that architectural coherence, implementation fit, and enacted fit are related but distinct. Coherent representation does not guarantee suitable configuration. Suitable configuration does not guarantee actualisation. Actualisation does not guarantee a beneficial or evenly distributed outcome.

## Part III: Complete technical expansion

### 13. Keep feature, affordance, actualisation, and outcome separate

| Concept | Minimal analytical statement | Evidence needed | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feature or material arrangement | The system contains or represents X | Technical or documentary inspection | Calling X an affordance without naming an actor and action |
| Affordance | X creates a possibility for actor A to perform action B under conditions C | Relational analysis; perception evidence strengthens the claim | Treating possibility as use |
| Constraint | X limits actor A's pursuit of goal G under conditions C | Technical and situated evidence | Treating every rule as a negative organisational effect |
| Actualisation | A takes actions over time to realise the possibility | Process and longitudinal evidence | Inferring actualisation from availability or adoption |
| Outcome | Consequence O emerges at level L over period T | Outcome evidence connected to the process | Attributing O directly to X |

### 14. Human and material agency must not be confused

Leonardi's account distinguishes human agency from material agency. Human actors form intentions and pursue goals. Material agency refers to the capacity of material arrangements to make a difference in action. This does not grant software human intention, moral responsibility, or legitimate authority.

An automated control can stop a transaction. It cannot, on that fact alone, justify the policy, accept accountability, interpret the wider purpose, or bear responsibility for harm. These questions require decision theory, governance, law, ethics, and empirical organisational evidence.

### 15. Encoded architecture can be analysed, but only at its own level

The documentary corpus can support analysis of:

- represented action possibilities and prohibitions;
- technical dependencies among roles, records, rules, and events;
- mandatory and optional data relations;
- visibility and traceability designed into records;
- configured or configurable variation points;
- automation boundaries and required human interventions; and
- candidate affordances and constraints stated conditionally.

It cannot establish:

- what users perceive;
- whether the arrangement is trusted or understood;
- whether formal roles carry practical authority;
- how work is distributed across system and non-system channels;
- whether recurrent use reproduces or changes organisational structures;
- whether an affordance is actualised; or
- whether an observed outcome was caused by the represented arrangement.

### 16. Enterprise-software propositions for later empirical testing

The following are research propositions generated by the synthesis. They are not presented as settled findings.

**P1: Visibility.** A shared record will afford cross-functional visibility only where relevant actors can access, interpret, and act on its contents within their work arrangements.

**P2: Workflow.** A workflow will afford coordinated progression only where routing logic, role occupancy, timing, exception handling, and local authority align sufficiently for actualisation.

**P3: Permission.** A permission will afford action to an actor without necessarily establishing accepted authority, accountability, or willingness to act.

**P4: Validation.** A validation may afford data consistency while constraining local exception handling, creating incentives for delay, escalation, or off-system work.

**P5: Automation.** Automation may relocate discretion and attention rather than remove them, shifting judgement toward rule design, exception classification, monitoring, and override.

**P6: Auditability.** Durable records may afford traceability without producing answerability or effective control unless governance arrangements connect records to review and consequence.

**P7: Bundles.** Organisational change will often depend on bundles of interrelated affordances rather than on one isolated feature.

**P8: Temporal development.** Actualisation can modify routines, interpretations, data quality, and later possibilities, so affordance effects should be studied longitudinally.

### 17. Method-to-claim matrix

| Method or source | Strongest defensible claim | What remains unavailable |
|---|---|---|
| Reference-process and product documentation | Represented arrangements and candidate action possibilities | Configuration, perception, actualisation, practice, and outcome |
| Configuration inspection | Selected technical rules and possibilities | Recurrent use and organisational consequence |
| Interviews | Actor accounts of goals, perceptions, constraints, use, and outcomes | Complete practice or causal effect without corroboration |
| Observation and shadowing | Situated sociomaterial action and workarounds | Long-term recurrence unless repeated over time |
| Event logs and process mining | Recorded sequences, timing, recurrence, and deviations | Unlogged talk, interpretation, manual work, and meaning |
| Longitudinal case study | Actualisation processes, adaptation, and changing effects | Broad generalisation without comparative design |
| Comparative case study | Variation across configurations and contexts | Universal causal claims without stronger design |
| Outcome measures | Changes in selected indicators | Mechanism and attribution unless linked to process evidence |

### 18. Cross-stream boundary analysis

**Organisation design:** Stream One asks how work, roles, information, decisions, and coordination arrangements fit together. Stream Six asks how technical arrangements become consequential in situated practice.

**Coordination:** Stream Two identifies dependencies and mechanisms. Stream Six prevents a represented mechanism from being treated as an achieved coordination outcome.

**Decision theory:** Stream Three explains premises, programmes, authority, and discretion. Stream Six shows how technical arrangements may participate in distributing or constraining action without equating technical agency with human judgement.

**Enterprise systems:** Stream Four explains package architecture, implementation, fit, and adaptation. Stream Six supplies relational and process explanations between configuration and outcome.

**Organisational routines:** Stream Five distinguishes artefacts, ostensive understandings, and performative patterns. Stream Six explains the material participation of technologies in recurrent action while preserving the need for evidence of practice.

**Digital infrastructure:** Stream Seven will extend materiality across installed bases, standards, organisations, and time.

**Governance and control:** Stream Eight will determine when permissions, records, monitoring, interventions, and consequences form legitimate and accountable governance arrangements.

**Business-process management:** Stream Nine will clarify the process representations through which many technical arrangements become visible to this study.

### 19. Prohibited inferences

- A documented feature is not automatically an affordance.
- A candidate affordance is not evidence of perception or actualisation.
- A technical constraint is not automatically a harmful organisational outcome.
- Availability is not adoption, and adoption is not recurrent use.
- Use is not proof of intended use or beneficial effect.
- A permission is not accepted authority.
- An automated action is not human intention or accountability.
- A system record is not the whole sociomaterial practice.
- A configured workflow is not an enacted routine.
- Technical traceability is not answerability.
- Architectural coherence is not implementation fit or enacted fit.
- One healthcare, engineering, or service case does not establish a universal enterprise-software mechanism.

### 20. Strongest defensible contribution to the project

Stream Six allows the project to take technology seriously without becoming technologically deterministic. It establishes a disciplined path from material arrangement to relational possibility, perception, actualisation, and outcome. It also shows exactly where the current documentary method must stop.

For Paper One, the strongest contribution is a bounded reconstruction of represented technical and organisational arrangements, including carefully labelled candidate affordances and constraints. For later implementation and enactment studies, the stream provides the concepts and evidence requirements needed to examine how configured technologies enter practice and with what consequences.

## Part IV: Audit conclusion

The evidence supports five conclusions.

1. Technology is both shaped through action and implicated in the conditions of later action.
2. Material arrangements matter without determining one organisational effect.
3. Affordances and constraints are relational and should not be reduced to feature labels.
4. Actualisation is the empirical process that separates technological potential from organisational consequence.
5. Documentary architecture can support bounded claims about represented arrangements and candidate possibilities, but not claims about perception, enacted practice, or realised outcomes.

The supplied reports helped locate sources and sharpen the six-layer analytical chain. Their claims were not imported without checking. The resulting stream is therefore narrower in some places and stronger in others: it preserves materiality, practice, affordance, and sociomateriality while making every transition in the causal story answerable to an appropriate form of evidence.
