## Part I: Learning guide

### 1. The practical problem

Enterprise software contains process diagrams, workflow rules, role descriptions, forms, data objects, decision points, automated actions, and records. These artefacts can look like organisational routines because they describe recurring work. That resemblance creates a serious analytical risk.

A documented process is not the same thing as a routine being performed. A process model can specify how work is intended or enabled to proceed. It cannot show whether people understand the process in the same way, whether they follow it, how they adapt it, what they do outside the system, or whether a recognisable pattern is sustained over time.

Organisational-routines research gives the project a precise way to explain this boundary. Its central lesson is:

> A reference process is an artefact that represents a possible or prescribed pattern of action. It is neither a complete ostensive routine nor evidence of performative enactment.

This does not make documentary analysis weak. It makes its contribution exact. Documents can reveal encoded activities, sequences, roles, rules, variation points, hand-offs, and records. They cannot establish recurrent organisational performance.

### 2. Six concepts to master

#### Organisational routine

**Definition:** A routine is a repetitive, recognisable pattern of interdependent actions carried out by multiple actors (Pentland and Haerem, 2015).

**Plain-language explanation:** A routine is not simply a written procedure or one repeated individual task. It is a pattern that becomes recognisable across repeated performances involving several actors whose actions depend on one another.

**Research use:** The definition prevents the project from calling every documented workflow a routine. SAP documentation can represent a candidate pattern of interdependent action. Only evidence of recurrent organisational performance can establish an enacted routine.

**Evidence boundary:** Recurrence, recognition, interdependence in action, and participation by multiple actors cannot be demonstrated from a vendor reference diagram alone.

#### Ostensive aspect

**Definition:** The ostensive aspect is the abstract or generalised idea of a routine to which participants refer when they guide, explain, or account for performances (Feldman and Pentland, 2003).

**Plain-language explanation:** It is what participants understand the routine to be, not simply what an official document says it should be.

**Research use:** A process model may become one resource through which an ostensive understanding is formed, communicated, or contested after implementation.

**Evidence boundary:** A document is not the ostensive aspect. Participants may interpret it differently, combine it with local knowledge, or ignore it. Interviews can provide accounts of ostensive understandings, but one account does not establish a shared organisational understanding.

#### Performative aspect

**Definition:** The performative aspect consists of specific actions by specific people at particular times and places that bring a routine into practice (Feldman and Pentland, 2003).

**Plain-language explanation:** It is the routine as people actually perform it in situated episodes.

**Research use:** The concept identifies the level of evidence needed to study enacted work, including observation, detailed event evidence, and longitudinal inquiry.

**Evidence boundary:** A process diagram, configured workflow, interview account, or isolated event does not by itself demonstrate a recurrent performative pattern.

#### Routine artefact

**Definition:** An artefact is a physical or symbolic object associated with a routine, such as a rule, form, checklist, process model, software system, template, or record.

**Plain-language explanation:** Artefacts can describe, prompt, constrain, enable, or record action. They are not interchangeable with the action pattern itself.

**Research use:** SAP reference-process material is best treated as an artefact of process design. Its activities, objects, roles, and rules can be analysed as represented organisational arrangements.

**Evidence boundary:** The presence of an artefact does not establish how it is interpreted, used, bypassed, or combined with other artefacts.

#### Generative system

**Definition:** A routine can be understood as a system capable of producing multiple recognisable performances rather than as one fixed sequence (Pentland and Rueter, 1994; Pentland and Feldman, 2005).

**Plain-language explanation:** The same routine need not look identical every time. Its performances may vary while remaining recognisable as instances of the same organisational accomplishment.

**Research use:** This concept prevents a standard process path from being treated as the only possible form of the work.

**Evidence boundary:** A branching process model represents designed alternatives. It does not establish the full range or distribution of performances generated in practice.

#### Routine dynamics

**Definition:** Routine dynamics studies how recognisable patterns of action emerge, persist, vary, interact, and change through repeated performance.

**Plain-language explanation:** Stability is something people and artefacts repeatedly accomplish. Change can emerge from the routine itself rather than only from an external redesign project.

**Research use:** The perspective explains why implementation cannot be treated as the final translation of a reference process into a stable organisation.

**Evidence boundary:** Routine change requires evidence across performances and time. A change between two documentation releases establishes a change in represented architecture, not a change in organisational routine.

### 3. The three-level distinction

| Analytical object | What it is | Evidence that can establish it | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Process artefact | A document, model, rule, system feature, template, or record | Documentary and technical evidence | The routine itself |
| Ostensive aspect | Participants' abstract and potentially multiple understandings of a routine | Participant accounts, talk, explanation, and interpretive evidence | An official process model |
| Performative aspect | Situated actions through which participants enact the routine | Observation, detailed event evidence, and repeated performance traces | A prescription or isolated event |

Artefacts may influence both aspects. Participants may use a process model to guide or justify action. Software may enable some sequences and block others. Records may make performances visible to later participants. None of these relations eliminates the analytical distinction.

### 4. A short reading route

1. Read Nelson and Winter (1982), then Becker (2004), to understand the evolutionary and capability tradition and the ambiguities surrounding the routines concept.
2. Read Pentland and Rueter (1994) for routines as grammars that generate varied but recognisable performances.
3. Read Feldman (2000) and Feldman and Pentland (2003) for agency, endogenous change, and the ostensive-performative distinction.
4. Read Pentland and Feldman (2005) for the routine as a unit with internal parts and relations.
5. Read Pentland and Feldman (2008), D'Adderio (2008, 2011), and Cacciatori (2012) for artefacts, software, and systems of artefacts.
6. Read Howard-Grenville (2005), Turner and Rindova (2012), and Sele and Grand (2016) for persistence, multiplicity, connections, and routine ecologies.
7. Read Feldman et al. (2016) and Feldman et al. (2021) for the mature routine-dynamics programme and its methodological implications.

## Part II: Readable scholarly synthesis

### 5. From stable repository to recurrent accomplishment

Nelson and Winter (1982) made routines central to an evolutionary theory of the firm. Routines store and reproduce organisational capability, provide continuity, and help explain firm behaviour without assuming continuous optimisation. This tradition made routines analytically important, but it also left an ambiguity noted by later reviews: the word routine could refer to a disposition or capability, to stored knowledge, or to an observable pattern of behaviour.

Cohen et al. (1996) exposed this ambiguity rather than solving it by definition. Their review asks how recurring action patterns relate to cognition, evolution, simulation, and empirical research. Becker (2004) later confirmed that the literature contained multiple definitions and effects attributed to routines. These sources are important because they show that the field did not move from one settled definition to another. It developed partly by separating questions that had previously been bundled together.

Pentland and Rueter (1994) supplied an early bridge toward a process view. Their study found repetitive, functionally similar patterns in work characterised by exceptions and deliberation. The grammar metaphor explains how a routine can generate multiple performances instead of prescribing one invariant sequence. It therefore weakens the equation between routine and standard operating procedure.

Feldman (2000) moved agency and reflection into the centre of the explanation. Her empirical analysis shows participants responding to outcomes of prior iterations, creating an internal dynamic through which routines can change continuously. Stability and change no longer have to be assigned to separate phenomena. Both may arise through repeated accomplishment.

**Section takeaway:** A routine is not a stored script whose existence can be established by locating a document. It is a recurrent organisational phenomenon whose pattern, participants, interpretations, and performances require empirical examination.

### 6. Ostensive and performative aspects explain stability and change

Feldman and Pentland (2003) distinguish the ostensive and performative aspects of a routine. The ostensive aspect is the abstract, generalised idea of the routine. Participants use it to guide, refer to, and account for performances. The performative aspect consists of situated actions by particular people at particular times and places.

The distinction does not create two independent objects. Performances create, maintain, and modify ostensive understandings. Ostensive understandings orient performances without mechanically determining them. This recursive relationship explains why routines may remain recognisable while their performances vary.

Multiplicity matters. Turner and Rindova (2012) show that participants may maintain more than one ostensive pattern, including simultaneous expectations of consistency and flexibility. Their qualitative study of six waste-management organisations also demonstrates that artefacts and connections help sustain these patterns. It is therefore unsafe to assume a single shared understanding from an official process statement.

Howard-Grenville (2005) adds context, agency, intention, and relative power. Flexible performances may persist as a recognisable routine because organisational context constrains ongoing adaptation, and because some participants have more influence over change than others. This complicates any claim that formal role assignment or technical permission establishes practical authority over a routine.

**Project interpretation:** A vendor reference process is not the ostensive aspect of a customer's routine. Before implementation, it is a vendor artefact representing a possible or preferred arrangement. After implementation, it may become one resource among others through which participants form and contest ostensive understandings.

### 7. Artefacts matter without becoming routines

Pentland and Feldman (2005) treat routines as generative systems with internal structure and propose analysing the whole routine, individual parts, or relations among parts. Artefacts are analytically important because they can represent aspects of a routine and participate in its performance, but they should not be substituted for the ostensive or performative aspect.

Pentland and Feldman (2008) make the design problem explicit. Managers often design forms, rules, flowcharts, checklists, and software while hoping to create patterns of coordinated action. Their failed-software example shows why artefact-centred assumptions are insufficient. Designing an artefact does not ensure the repetitive, recognisable, interdependent action pattern intended by the designer.

D'Adderio (2003) demonstrates why software nevertheless has organisational force. Codification and delegation of organisational memory to integrated software transform knowledge and routines as they are reconfigured into computer-embedded representations. Her later work rejects both extremes: artefacts are neither fully determining nor inconsequential. D'Adderio (2008) describes iterative relations between formal rules and actual performances as framing, overflowing, and reframing. D'Adderio (2011) places artefacts and materiality at the centre of routine dynamics while preserving their mutual relation with performance.

Cacciatori (2012) extends the focus from isolated artefacts to systems of artefacts. In a case of a new bidding routine, different artefacts were bundled in ways that reinforced patterns of action and helped work out conflict and truce. This matters for enterprise systems because a routine is rarely related to one screen or workflow alone. Reference diagrams, configuration, master data, authorisations, interfaces, local procedures, spreadsheets, and records may form an interacting artefact system.

**Section takeaway:** The correct alternative to technological determinism is not to treat software as powerless. Software can inscribe categories, require inputs, automate transitions, restrict actions, and produce records. The empirical question is how those capacities enter routine performance, not whether the artefact is the routine.

### 8. Stability is patterned accomplishment, not identical repetition

The practice perspective changes what stability means. A stable routine need not reproduce an identical sequence on every occasion. It must exhibit enough recurrence and recognition for participants and analysts to identify a pattern.

Pentland and Haerem (2015) synthesise this position by defining routines as repetitive, recognisable patterns of interdependent action carried out by multiple actors in social and material contexts. Pentland, Haerem, and Hillison (2010) show how recurrent action patterns can be compared through sequence-sensitive methods. Pentland et al. (2012) develop a generative model in which variation and selective retention of action patterns can explain formation, inertia, endogenous change, and learning.

Turner and Rindova (2012) show that consistency and flexibility may be pursued together rather than treated as opposites. Howard-Grenville (2005) similarly demonstrates how agency and context produce both flexible performance and persistence.

These arguments change the interpretation of exceptions. A documented exception is a designed variation path. It cannot establish how often the path is used, whether actors regard it as part of the routine, or whether undocumented variation occurs elsewhere. A deviation from a process model is not automatically an error. It may be an authorised exception, situated adjustment, workaround, innovation, or evidence that the documented path was never the operative pattern.

### 9. Routines interact in ecologies

End-to-end enterprise processes are assembled from linked activities, roles, data objects, controls, and subprocesses. Routine theory warns against treating these elements as one isolated routine.

Sele and Grand (2016) study how human and nonhuman actors connect routines in a research laboratory. Their analysis distinguishes intermediaries that maintain connections from mediators that modify connections and generate adaptation, new performances, or innovative outcomes. The contribution is not a general law of enterprise integration. It is a mechanism-sensitive account of how routine interactions can become generative.

Feldman et al. (2016) describe relationality as a major development in routine dynamics. Analysis may need to move beyond one routine toward relations and networks of routines. For P2P, O2C, and R2R, this means that the apparent process boundary in vendor documentation is an analytical boundary, not proof of an enacted routine boundary.

**Project interpretation:** Cross-process master data, shared records, hand-offs, and event triggers can be coded as represented links among possible action patterns. Whether they form an enacted ecology of routines requires organisational evidence.

### 10. Enterprise software sits between representation and performance

D'Adderio's integrated-systems research and Pentland and Feldman's software-design argument provide the strongest direct bridge to this project. Beverungen (2014) adds a business-process perspective by treating business processes as emergent routines that may be represented, enabled, and constrained by IT artefacts. His meta-framework directly challenges purely top-down accounts of process design.

Yamauchi and Swanson (2010) study local assimilation of a CRM system in regional bank branches through observation and interviews. Users developed familiarity pockets within which routine system use became possible while much of the wider system remained outside their working knowledge. The study is valuable evidence that routine use of an enterprise system can coexist with limited understanding of the system as a whole. It is not an SAP, ERP, public-cloud, or reference-architecture study.

These sources support a layered model:

1. **Represented process:** the vendor artefact and the organisational possibilities or prescriptions it depicts.
2. **Configured process:** the customer-specific technical and organisational arrangement selected during implementation.
3. **Ostensive understandings:** participants' potentially multiple ideas of how the recurrent work proceeds.
4. **Performative patterns:** the recurrent actions through which the work is accomplished.
5. **Outcomes:** consequences attributed to those performances in a specified setting and time.

Each level may influence the next, but none establishes it. A configured workflow remains an artefact. It may strongly constrain possible system actions, but organisational performance can include interpretation, timing, communication, preparation, exception handling, and off-system activity not captured by the configured sequence.

### 11. The candidate formulation needs one refinement

The review supports the original formulation, with a change that avoids describing an unimplemented vendor artefact as an organisational routine:

> An enterprise-software reference process is a designed artefact that represents a possible or prescribed pattern of coordinated action. Through selection, configuration, interpretation, and use, it may become one resource in the formation of ostensive understandings and performative patterns. It is not itself a complete ostensive routine and cannot establish an enacted routine.

The phrase **possible or prescribed** matters. Some elements depict alternatives that a customer may select. Others state required sequence or technical preconditions within the represented design. The phrase **one resource** matters because local procedures, professional knowledge, managerial direction, law, informal coordination, and other artefacts may also shape the routine.

The formulation must not be weakened into the claim that software merely suggests action. Implemented software may block transactions, assign access, calculate values, create records, or trigger automated action. These are material constraints and capabilities. What remains unestablished is how the wider recurrent organisational work is performed.

## Part III: Complete technical expansion

### 12. Conceptual genealogy and continuing tension

The evolutionary and practice perspectives ask related but different questions. Nelson and Winter (1982) use routines to explain organisational continuity, capability, search, and economic evolution. The practice perspective opens the routine and examines the actions through which recurrence, stability, and change are accomplished.

Parmigiani and Howard-Grenville (2011) organise this division as capabilities and practice perspectives. The capabilities perspective commonly treats routines as organisational building blocks associated with reliable performance. The practice perspective focuses on internal dynamics, situated performances, and the relation between stability and change. The perspectives should not be collapsed. The first helps explain why routines matter for organisational capability. The second provides the stronger safeguard against treating process artefacts as performed organisation.

For Paper 1, the practice perspective performs the main analytical work because the data are reference artefacts. The capability perspective becomes relevant only as a boundary: documented functionality or a reference process cannot demonstrate organisational capability.

### 13. Ostensive evidence is more demanding than a procedure document

Feldman and Pentland's ostensive aspect is not equivalent to a process description. It is an abstract understanding that participants invoke in relation to performance. Several consequences follow:

- one document may support several participant interpretations;
- one routine may have multiple ostensive understandings;
- an official account may have more authority without being the only operative understanding;
- participants may account for the same performance through different routine narratives;
- an interview supplies an account, not transparent access to a single collective cognition.

Document analysis can identify the **official represented account**. It cannot label that account an ostensive routine without evidence that relevant participants use it to guide, refer to, or account for recurrent performances.

### 14. Performative evidence requires pattern as well as action

Observation of one event can show action, but a routine is recurrent and recognisable. Evidence of the performative aspect therefore requires attention to multiple performances, their interdependence, and the basis on which they count as instances of the same routine.

Event logs can contribute by providing traces of recorded system events across many cases. They remain partial. A log records what the system was designed and configured to record. It may omit conversations, preparation, embodied action, informal coordination, off-system work, and reasons for variation. Process mining can analyse recorded patterns and conformance. It should not be described as reconstructing the complete performative routine.

Interviews can provide accounts of specific performances and participant interpretations. Observation and ethnography can reveal situated action and artefact use. Longitudinal designs can examine stability and change. Strong routine research often combines methods because no single source establishes every aspect.

### 15. Artefact effects should be specified, not presumed

The project should avoid the undifferentiated statement that an artefact "shapes" a routine. Documentary analysis can specify the capacity represented in the artefact:

| Artefact capacity | Documentary observation | Later empirical question |
|---|---|---|
| Represent | Activities, roles, objects, sequence, and intended relation | Do participants recognise or use this representation? |
| Prescribe | Required step, rule, threshold, or responsibility | How is the prescription interpreted and enforced? |
| Enable | Available transaction, pathway, data, or integration | Is the capability configured and used? |
| Constrain | Prohibited action, mandatory field, authorisation, or sequence | What action is blocked, redirected, or moved outside the system? |
| Automate | System-performed calculation, creation, posting, or trigger | Does the automation operate as documented, and how do actors respond? |
| Record | Logged event, status, document, or audit field | What is recorded, omitted, corrected, or used later? |
| Connect | Hand-off, shared object, upstream trigger, or downstream dependency | How is the connection enacted and repaired? |

The left and middle columns belong to Paper 1. The right column defines later implementation and enactment research.

### 16. Coding implications for reference architecture

Routine theory justifies descriptive codes for the represented action pattern while imposing explicit limits.

| Proposed code | Observable in reference evidence | Theoretical relevance | Prohibited inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Represented recurrence | Process described as repeatable or periodic | Candidate recurrent pattern | That recurrence occurs in an organisation |
| Prescribed sequence | Ordered activities or required predecessor | Artefact representation of temporal pattern | That the sequence is enacted |
| Role allocation | Named role linked to activity or decision | Represented participant distribution | That the role exists or authority is accepted |
| Trigger | Event or condition that begins a path | Represented initiation relation | That actors recognise or respond to it |
| Rule | Conditional or mandatory relation | Prescriptive artefact | That it is followed or legitimate |
| Variation point | Branch or documented alternative | Designed patterned variation | That actual variation occurs only there |
| Exception path | Named departure from standard path | Designed exception handling | Frequency, acceptability, or completeness |
| Automated action | System performs an action or transition | Material participation in represented process | Reliable execution or removal of human discretion |
| Record | Object or trace created or updated | Representation and visibility of action | Completeness, accuracy, or later use |
| Feedback | Represented review or corrective loop | Potential retention and change mechanism | Learning or routine change |
| Hand-off | Transfer among roles or process areas | Represented interdependence | Effective coordination |
| Routine link | Process-to-process or object-mediated connection | Candidate routine ecology | Enacted routine boundary or generative effect |

These codes should retain the prefix **represented** wherever a reader could otherwise mistake them for empirical findings about organisational routines.

### 17. Relationship with organisation design, coordination, and decision theory

Routine theory does not replace the first three streams.

- Organisation design identifies represented differentiation, integration, roles, information-processing arrangements, and configurations.
- Coordination theory identifies represented dependencies and mechanisms.
- Decision theory identifies decision premises, programmes, permissions, authority claims, and discretion.
- Routine theory explains why these represented arrangements cannot be treated as recurrent organisational performance.

The same process artefact may therefore receive several non-duplicative interpretations. A mandatory approval may be an encoded coordination mechanism, a decision programme, a control candidate, and a routine artefact. Routine theory contributes the artefact-performance boundary rather than another label for approval.

### 18. Boundary with technology and materiality

Stream 5 establishes that artefacts participate in routine dynamics without determining them. Stream 6 must go further by examining how technology becomes consequential through practice, material agency, affordance, constraint, and sociomaterial relations.

Leonardi (2011) is therefore referenced here only as a bridge. His analysis of affordance, constraint, and imbrication belongs primarily in Stream 6. Using it as the main explanation in Stream 5 would blur routine dynamics with the broader materiality literature.

### 19. Method-to-claim matrix

| Method or evidence | Strongest defensible contribution | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor documentation | Represented activities, roles, rules, objects, variants, and relations | No customer configuration or enactment |
| Configuration evidence | Customer-specific technical arrangement and enabled choice | No proof of recurrent use |
| Interviews | Participant accounts and interpretations of routine and performance | Recall, strategy, and no direct observation |
| Observation or ethnography | Situated performances, artefact use, informal action, and local variation | Limited coverage and possible observer effects |
| Event logs and process mining | Recorded event sequences, recurrence, variation, timing, and conformance | Only logged events; weak access to meaning and off-system work |
| Longitudinal mixed methods | Emergence, persistence, feedback, drift, and change across time | High access and resource demands |

### 20. Propositions for later empirical studies

These propositions are not findings of Paper 1.

**Proposition 1:** A reference process will influence participants' ostensive understandings more strongly when it is embedded in configured system constraints, training, local procedures, and recognised authority than when it remains a standalone vendor model.

**Proposition 2:** Divergence between represented process requirements and situated work demands will be associated with adaptations inside or outside the system, but the form of adaptation will depend on available discretion, visibility, and sanction.

**Proposition 3:** Repeated variations are more likely to alter ostensive understandings when they become visible, discussable, and retainable through organisational feedback and artefacts.

**Proposition 4:** Shared enterprise artefacts can connect routines across functions, but the effects of those connections depend on how actors enact, interpret, and repair the represented hand-offs.

**Proposition 5:** Greater technical enforcement may reduce variation in recorded system events while leaving substantial variation in preparation, communication, timing, interpretation, and off-system action.

### 21. Prohibited inferences

Reference-process documentation alone does not permit the claim that:

- the represented process is an organisational routine;
- the process model is the ostensive aspect of a routine;
- organisational actors share the vendor's process understanding;
- the process is recurrently performed;
- role occupants act as specified;
- a configured rule is accepted, legitimate, or consistently followed;
- an automated step eliminates judgement or discretion;
- logged events capture the complete work;
- a documented exception is the only form of variation;
- standardisation in the artefact produces uniform performance;
- process conformance produces coordination quality or organisational capability;
- an artefact change caused routine or organisational change.

### 22. Strongest defensible contribution to the project

Routine scholarship is not evidence that SAP customers depart from standard processes. It is a theory-led reason not to infer either conformity or departure from reference architecture.

Its main contribution is methodological:

> Vendor reference artefacts can support a bounded reconstruction of represented organisational logic, but routine claims begin only when recurrent, recognisable, interdependent action and participant understandings are studied in an organisational setting.

This boundary strengthens rather than diminishes Paper 1. The paper can analyse what organisation is encoded without pretending to observe what organisation is enacted.

## Part IV: Audit conclusion

The LeapSpace report contains useful leads to Feldman and Pentland, D'Adderio, Howard-Grenville, routine ecologies, and workflow-log research. Its synthesis cannot be accepted because it cites the prompt as evidence, contains unexplained gaps in its reference numbering, includes unrelated publications, and declares high confidence without an adequate access audit.

The SciSpace report is broader and correctly foregrounds the artefact, ostensive, and performative distinction. It also identifies valuable sources on artefact systems, routine ecology, business-process emergence, and enterprise-system assimilation. Its numbered citations are nevertheless unreliable: many numbers in the prose resolve to different works in the final reference list, and several foundational works discussed in the prose are absent from that numbered list. Claims about cloud ERP, complete reconstruction of performance through logs, and a general represented-configured-enacted framework were narrowed or rebuilt from other streams.

The resulting review retains the reports' useful discovery work but rests its argument on independently verified publications. The review also preserves strict boundaries with Stream 4 on implementation and Stream 6 on technology and materiality.
