# Scholarly Writing and Teaching Standard

## Purpose

Project writing must remain rigorous while being enjoyable to read and useful for teaching. Accessibility is not simplification by deletion. Every rewrite must preserve the substantive claims, qualifications, evidence boundaries, citations, rival interpretations, and unresolved verification issues in the source material.

## Intended reader

Write for an intelligent reader who understands organisations or enterprise systems but may not already know the theoretical literature. Introduce specialised vocabulary before relying on it. Never require the reader to infer why an author, concept, or distinction has entered the argument.

## Standard reading architecture

Long theoretical reviews should normally offer three layers:

1. **Learning guide:** the practical problem, plain-language concepts, connections among theorists, examples, and a reading route.
2. **Readable scholarly synthesis:** the complete argument in a clear sequence, with transitions, section takeaways, and explicit evidential limits.
3. **Technical expansion:** detailed source discussion, debates, coding implications, propositions, and verification notes.

The consolidated literature review and book chapters need not reproduce these headings mechanically. They should provide the same progression from orientation to explanation to scholarly depth.

## Paragraph and section method

Each paragraph should perform one main job. A theoretical section should normally answer these questions in order:

1. What practical or analytical problem is at issue?
2. What does the concept mean in plain language?
3. Which author supplies or changes the concept?
4. How does this contribution relate to the preceding literature?
5. How does the concept help this research?
6. What can the available evidence not establish?

Use short orienting sentences before dense passages. Explain relationships among authors instead of presenting a sequence of names. Use tables only when they make comparisons or mappings easier to understand.

## Concept explanation pattern

For an important concept, use this sequence where appropriate:

- **Definition:** State what the concept means.
- **Plain-language explanation:** Explain the underlying idea without jargon.
- **Illustration:** Provide a concrete, clearly labelled example.
- **Research use:** State what the concept lets the project observe or analyse.
- **Boundary:** State what cannot be inferred.

Examples must never be written as empirical findings unless they are supported by registered project evidence.

## Claim labels

Use these labels when the status of a passage could otherwise be unclear:

- **Established claim:** Directly supported by identified scholarship.
- **Project interpretation:** A reasoned application of scholarship to the research problem.
- **Illustrative example:** A teaching device, not a finding.
- **Proposition:** A claim to be examined through data.
- **Evidence boundary:** A statement of what the material cannot establish.
- **Verification required:** A source or bibliographic matter still needing confirmation.

## Style rules

- Prefer concrete subjects and active verbs.
- Define a technical term on first use.
- Avoid strings of abstract nouns when a direct sentence is possible.
- Keep citations close to the claims they support.
- Use signposting to explain why a section follows from the previous one.
- Distinguish software capability, encoded arrangement, implemented configuration, enacted practice, and organisational outcome.
- Do not use em dashes. Use commas, parentheses, colons, or full stops.
- Do not make prose conversational at the expense of precision.

## Preservation audit

After a readability rewrite, confirm that it preserves:

- every material theoretical distinction;
- all qualifications and counterarguments;
- evidence and access limitations;
- the encoded, implemented, and enacted boundary;
- propositions and coding implications;
- citation coverage and verification flags;
- disagreements or tensions between sources.

If a concept is too detailed for the main narrative, move it to a technical subsection, note, table, or appendix. Do not silently remove it.

## Standard for the consolidated review

The consolidated review should read as one cumulative argument, not as summaries placed beside one another. Each stream should enter because it resolves a limitation left by the preceding stream. Transitions should make that intellectual work explicit. The final synthesis should show what each theory explains, what it leaves unresolved, and why the combined framework is necessary.

## Standard for the book

Book chapters should teach before they compress. Open with the organisational problem, use a process or artefact to make the problem tangible, explain theory in the order needed, and return to the example with sharper analytical language. Each chapter should give practitioners usable questions while preserving the evidential discipline expected by scholarly readers.
