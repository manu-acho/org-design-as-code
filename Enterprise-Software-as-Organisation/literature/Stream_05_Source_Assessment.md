# Stream Five: Source Assessment

## Assessment of the supplied LeapSpace report

The ten-page LeapSpace report is a discovery aid, not a completed or reliable literature review.

Major defects:

1. References 1, 3, 6, and 7 point to the uploaded prompt rather than scholarship.
2. The report's numbered sequence begins with missing and duplicated numbers, making coverage impossible to audit from the numbering alone.
3. Several requested foundational works, including Nelson and Winter (1982), Pentland and Rueter (1994), Cohen et al. (1996), and Parmigiani and Howard-Grenville (2011), are not properly represented in the usable bibliography.
4. The report claims high confidence without stating whether sources were read in full, by abstract, or through metadata.
5. Sources on ecological knowledge, community participation, forest policy, nuclear maintenance culture, and IFRS interpretation are used to support generic methodological claims even though they are not organisational-routines evidence for this project.
6. The report treats process mining and event logs too simply as evidence of enactment without acknowledging that logs contain only recorded system events.
7. It calls D'Adderio's integrated-system study ERP research without establishing that product and setting from the accessible source.
8. It does not deliver the requested source audit, claim-to-source table, complete conceptual distinctions, or access verification.

Useful leads retained after independent checking include Feldman and Pentland (2003), Feldman (2000), Pentland and Feldman (2005, 2008), D'Adderio (2003, 2008, 2011), Howard-Grenville (2005), Pentland et al. (2012), Turner and Rindova (2012), and Sele and Grand (2016).

## Assessment of the supplied SciSpace report

The twenty-seven-page SciSpace report is more substantial, but it cannot be cited or imported as evidence.

Material problems:

1. The in-text reference numbers frequently do not correspond to the works listed under those numbers in the final reference list. For example, passages attributing the ostensive-performative framework to references 7 and 56 resolve to Iwao (2015) and Pentland, Haerem, and Hillison (2010), not Feldman and Pentland (2003).
2. The final numbered references omit or misroute several foundational works that the prose discusses, including Nelson and Winter (1982), Pentland and Rueter (1994), Feldman (2000), and Feldman and Pentland (2003).
3. The report labels many items "full text" without providing an auditable basis for that access claim.
4. It describes process mining as reconstructing the performative aspect. Event logs can reveal recorded action sequences, not the complete situated performance.
5. It says documentary evidence cannot establish either routine aspect, which is broadly correct, but then sometimes treats interviews as establishing a collective ostensive aspect. Interviews provide situated accounts that may differ among participants.
6. The cloud-ERP gap section claims reduced customisation and frequent vendor updates using citations that do not support those product-specific claims. Those questions belong primarily to Stream 4 and remain empirically open.
7. It presents the represented-configured-enacted distinction as if it came directly from routines scholarship. The distinction is a project synthesis across Streams 4 to 6 and must be labelled accordingly.
8. It treats D'Adderio (2003) as an ERP study. The verified abstract concerns integrated systems and computer-embedded representations but does not justify a narrower ERP label.
9. It misstates Yamauchi and Swanson (2010) as a four-author study and gives the wrong page range. The verified publication is by Yutaka Yamauchi and E. Burton Swanson, pages 187-206, and studies a CRM system in regional bank branches.
10. It misidentifies the Bartoszewicz and Susabowska (2014) publication as a mainstream *Human Resource Management* article at pages 745-765. The verified institutional record places it in *Zarzadzanie Zasobami Ludzkimi*, issue 6, with records varying between pages 67-83 and 69-86. The original publication is in Polish with an English summary.
11. Several propositions in the report introduce moderators and causal relations not established by the cited sources. They may be research questions, but they cannot be presented as literature findings.
12. The report reproduces prompt language about SAP and research gaps without supplying independent SAP-specific routine evidence.

Useful additions retained after verification include Beverungen (2014), Cacciatori (2012), Feldman et al. (2016, 2021), Sele and Grand (2016), Turner and Rindova (2012), and Yamauchi and Swanson (2010).

## Claims explicitly rejected or narrowed

- **Rejected:** A process model is an ostensive routine. It is an artefact and may become a resource for participant understandings.
- **Rejected:** Event logs reconstruct the complete performative routine. They reconstruct recorded event traces.
- **Rejected:** Interviews establish a single collective ostensive aspect. They provide situated and potentially conflicting accounts.
- **Rejected:** D'Adderio (2003) is verified as an ERP case. The accessible source supports integrated systems and embedded representations, not the narrower product label.
- **Rejected:** Routines theory proves that cloud ERP reduces customer discretion. Product and implementation claims belong to Stream 4 and require product-specific evidence.
- **Rejected:** Standard reference processes produce standardised organisational performance.
- **Narrowed:** Software can perform automated actions and strongly constrain technical possibilities, but the wider organisational routine remains an empirical question.
- **Narrowed:** Process mining can identify recorded recurrence, variation, conformance, and timing, but cannot recover unlogged meaning, communication, or off-system activity.
- **Narrowed:** Routine ecology is useful for cross-process analysis, but documented process links are only representations of possible routine relations.

## Verification priorities

1. Read the assigned edition of Nelson and Winter (1982) before using page-specific claims.
2. Obtain full text for Cohen et al. (1996), Becker (2004), and Pentland and Feldman (2005).
3. Verify detailed uses of D'Adderio (2011), Parmigiani and Howard-Grenville (2011), and Pentland et al. (2012) against complete text.
4. Retrieve Glaser (2017) before using design-performance mechanisms.
5. Review the methods chapters of the *Cambridge Handbook of Routine Dynamics* before finalising later enactment studies.
6. Keep cloud ERP and Fit-to-Standard propositions in Stream 4 unless an organisational-routines study directly supports them.
7. Treat the supplied reports as private discovery artefacts and do not add them to the scholarly bibliography.
