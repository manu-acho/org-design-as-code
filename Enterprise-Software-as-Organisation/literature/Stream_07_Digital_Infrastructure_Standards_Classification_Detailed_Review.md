# Stream Seven: Digital Infrastructure, Standards, and Classification

## Scope and evidence boundary

This review examines how information systems become infrastructure, how standards hold distributed arrangements together, and how classifications organise what can be recorded, compared, exchanged, and acted upon. It draws on verified publisher records, complete open or repository manuscripts, and clearly identified abstract-level evidence. Detailed claims are restricted to the versions actually inspected.

The review does not assume that SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition is an infrastructure merely because it is large or widely used. A bounded software package, a configured enterprise system, an organisation's application landscape, and a cross-organisational digital infrastructure are different analytical objects. Infrastructural status is relational and empirical. It depends on how an arrangement becomes embedded in practice, connected with other systems, taken for granted, shared across settings, and sustained over time.

## Part I: Learning guide

### 1. The practical problem

Enterprise software presents itself through visible applications, process diagrams, roles, records, interfaces, and configuration options. Yet many of its most consequential arrangements sit in the background. Product identifiers allow transactions to connect. Organisational units define where rules apply. Document types classify events. Interfaces link one system to another. Release conventions and extension rules determine how change can occur without breaking dependent arrangements.

These elements do more than store information. They help create the conditions under which distributed work can be coordinated. Their effects are rarely confined to a single screen or process step. A classification introduced for accounting may shape procurement, reporting, analytics, authorisation, and later automation. A local change may therefore encounter dependencies accumulated across many systems and practices.

Stream Seven explains this background organisation through five linked ideas:

1. infrastructure is relational rather than merely technical;
2. an installed base gives new design both resources and constraints;
3. standards enable connection while creating dependencies and exclusions;
4. classifications are operational and political, not neutral labels; and
5. digital infrastructures evolve through distributed, path-dependent, and partly generative processes.

### 2. Concepts to master

#### Relational infrastructure

Star and Ruhleder (1996) argue that infrastructure is not simply a thing with stable properties. It becomes infrastructure in relation to organised practice. The same arrangement can be background infrastructure for one group and a visible object of work for another.

This is why scale alone is insufficient. A global ERP product is not automatically infrastructure for every customer. In one organisation, its identifiers, records, interfaces, and routines may become deeply embedded in daily work. In another, it may remain one application among several, surrounded by spreadsheets, manual handoffs, and alternative systems.

#### Installed base

An installed base consists of existing technical components, standards, data, practices, skills, institutions, and organisational commitments on which new arrangements must build. Hanseth, Monteiro, and Hatling (1996) show why infrastructure development is pulled between standardisation and flexibility. Compatibility stabilises connection, but extensive interdependence makes change costly.

The installed base is therefore not technical debt in a narrow sense. It is the accumulated environment of previous choices. New design is rarely performed on empty ground.

#### Standards

Standards make heterogeneous activities comparable and connectable. Timmermans and Epstein (2010) describe standardisation as an attempt to create equivalence across time, geography, and social settings. Standards may prescribe formats, categories, measurements, interfaces, procedures, or expected outputs.

Their effects are contingent. A standard may reduce coordination costs, support interoperability, and make performance visible. It may also privilege some practices, displace local knowledge, or generate work needed to make apparently universal categories function locally.

#### Classification

Bowker and Star (1999) treat classifications as part of information infrastructure. Categories distribute visibility and organise action. They determine what counts as a case, where an item belongs, which comparisons become possible, and which residual cases become difficult to represent.

An enterprise-software code is therefore not merely descriptive. When it controls routing, valuation, access, reporting, or eligibility, classification becomes executable organisation. That statement concerns represented operational consequences. The legitimacy and lived effects of a classification require further evidence.

#### Path dependence

Infrastructure evolution depends on earlier choices. Existing standards, interfaces, skills, data structures, and network effects make some changes easier and others harder. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) describe information infrastructures as shared, open, heterogeneous, evolving sociotechnical systems whose development is nonlinear and path dependent.

Path dependence does not mean that change is impossible. It means that change takes place through an inherited arrangement rather than from a neutral starting point.

#### Generativity

Generativity concerns the capacity of digital arrangements to support unprompted or distributed innovation beyond the intentions of a single designer. Tilson, Lyytinen, and Sørensen (2010) connect digital infrastructure with generativity and changing control points. Henfridsson and Bygstad (2013) identify adoption, innovation, and scaling as mechanisms associated with infrastructure evolution, while emphasising contingent configurations rather than one universal sequence.

Generativity must not be treated as unlimited openness. Interfaces, extension rules, governance, installed-base dependencies, platform control, and resource inequalities shape who can innovate and under which conditions.

### 3. A short reading route

1. Read Star and Ruhleder (1996) for the relational character of infrastructure and the problem of access across organisational settings.
2. Read Bowker and Star (1999) for classification, standards, residual categories, and the politics embedded in information systems.
3. Read Hanseth, Monteiro, and Hatling (1996) for the tension between standardisation and flexibility.
4. Read Edwards et al. (2009) and Monteiro et al. (2013) for the shift from bounded artefacts to evolving infrastructures.
5. Read Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) for bootstrap, adaptability, path dependence, and design rules.
6. Read Tilson, Lyytinen, and Sørensen (2010) and Henfridsson and Bygstad (2013) for generativity and infrastructure evolution.
7. Read Timmermans and Epstein (2010) to resist simple accounts of standards as either domination or efficiency.
8. Read Plantin et al. (2018) to examine the convergence of platform and infrastructure logics.

### Study pause: is the object actually infrastructure?

For any SAP artefact, ask:

1. For whom is it background infrastructure, and for whom is it a visible object of work?
2. Which practices and other systems depend on it?
3. Which classifications or standards permit connection?
4. Which installed-base commitments constrain change?
5. Which actors can alter it, and through which governed mechanism?
6. What evidence shows embedded use rather than merely documented availability?

If the last question cannot be answered, describe the artefact as a candidate infrastructural component rather than an enacted infrastructure.

## Part II: Readable scholarly synthesis

### 4. Infrastructure is a relation of embeddedness

Star and Ruhleder's study of a collaborative system for a dispersed scientific community begins with a puzzle. The system received substantial design attention and positive reactions, yet many intended users still encountered difficulty joining and using it. The explanation could not be reduced to interface quality. Access depended on local resources, skills, support, conventions, work organisation, and wider changes in Internet use.

The case leads to a relational account. Infrastructure is learned as part of membership, linked with conventions of practice, built on an installed base, and usually becomes visible when it breaks down. It reaches beyond a single event or site and is shaped through local as well as wider arrangements.

This account changes how enterprise software should be analysed. The architectural corpus can identify common records, interfaces, classifications, roles, and cross-process dependencies. It cannot establish that those arrangements have become taken-for-granted infrastructure in a customer organisation. That claim requires evidence of embedded use, dependency, maintenance, breakdown, and adaptation.

### 5. Classification provides the hidden scaffolding

Bowker and Star (1999) show that categories and standards form scaffolding for information infrastructures. Classification makes heterogeneous phenomena administratively and technically tractable. It enables aggregation and comparison, but every classification also embodies choices about similarity, difference, relevance, and residual cases.

The importance of classification becomes clearer when categories are connected to action. A material type can determine which views, rules, valuation procedures, or planning processes apply. A document status can permit or block the next transition. An organisational code can define the scope of access, reporting, and responsibility. Categories thereby participate in allocating work and producing organisational visibility.

The analytical task is not to denounce classification as inherently oppressive or celebrate it as neutral order. It is to reconstruct what a category distinguishes, what actions depend on it, what cases fit poorly, and who can change the scheme. Consequences that reach beyond represented architecture must then be investigated empirically.

### 6. Standards produce both connection and work

Timmermans and Epstein (2010) define a sociological agenda for standards by emphasising their emergence, institutionalisation, implementation difficulties, resistance, and variable outcomes. Standards seek equivalence across settings, but equivalence must be accomplished. Local actors interpret requirements, reconcile conflicts, repair data, and adapt practices.

This point prevents two errors in enterprise-software research. The first assumes that a standard process produces identical work everywhere. The second assumes that local variation proves the failure of standardisation. In practice, standards and local work may be mutually dependent. A shared process can coordinate across units precisely because people perform the additional work needed to make local cases legible to it.

Reference architecture therefore provides evidence of a proposed common scheme. Implementation evidence is required to establish how that scheme was selected and configured. Enactment evidence is required to establish the work through which it is sustained, repaired, or resisted.

### 7. The installed base makes design evolutionary

Hanseth, Monteiro, and Hatling (1996) develop the tension between standardisation and flexibility. Information-infrastructure components are interdependent. Stability in one part may be needed so another can change. As connections accumulate, redesign becomes more difficult because a modification can propagate through dependent arrangements.

Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) extend this reasoning through the bootstrap and adaptability problems. An emerging infrastructure must be useful enough to attract early users, yet it must also remain adaptable under uncertain scale and future use. Their design theory favours simple initial solutions, modularity, generative capability, and strategies that cultivate rather than fully specify an evolving whole.

This is a different design problem from configuring a bounded workflow. SAP Public Edition provides governed scope, configuration, integration, and extension mechanisms. These may connect into a larger organisational infrastructure, but the product's bounded design authority should not be confused with the emergent and distributed control characteristic of broader information infrastructures.

### 8. Digital infrastructure changes the locus of control

Tilson, Lyytinen, and Sørensen (2010) argue that digital infrastructures deserve analysis as a distinct type of IT artefact. They emphasise relationality, generativity, large-scale change, and paradoxes of control. Infrastructure growth can increase the number and diversity of actors able to contribute, while standards, gateways, interfaces, owners, and regulators create control points.

Yoo, Henfridsson, and Lyytinen (2010) add layered modular architecture. Digital products can combine device, network, service, and content layers connected through standards and protocols. Components may participate in multiple design hierarchies, allowing recombination beyond a single product boundary.

These arguments are useful for cloud enterprise software only when applied carefully. Public APIs, released business objects, integration services, and extension environments may permit recombination. Yet the provider still controls core architecture, release policy, compatibility rules, and admissible extension points. The resulting arrangement may combine generativity with strong architectural governance.

### 9. Infrastructure evolves through contingent mechanisms

Henfridsson and Bygstad (2013) examine digital-infrastructure evolution through a critical-realist, configurational approach. Their in-depth case and survey of 41 cases identify adoption, innovation, and scaling as generative mechanisms. Different configurations can lead to successful evolution, and the authors do not claim to have identified every mechanism or every negative path.

This qualification matters. Generativity is not an outcome guaranteed by technical openness. Adoption can occur without valuable innovation. Innovation can create fragmentation. Scaling can intensify dependencies or move control toward a platform owner. Research on enterprise software should therefore examine combinations of mechanisms and conditions rather than treating extensibility as a direct measure of adaptability.

### 10. From artefact biography to infrastructural development

Monteiro et al. (2013) argue for moving from isolated artefacts toward infrastructures and their long-term development. Edwards et al. (2009) similarly describe infrastructure as unfinished, layered, historically accumulated, and difficult to study within one bounded site or moment.

This temporal orientation complements the distinction among architectural coherence, implementation fit, and enacted fit. A coherent reference architecture is a snapshot of represented relations at a controlled release. Infrastructure analysis asks how those relations connect with an installed base, change across releases, acquire dependants, and become maintained through organisational work.

Release control is therefore methodological rather than clerical. Without it, the analysis may silently combine categories, interfaces, and process arrangements that did not coexist. Longitudinal comparison should record when an element entered, changed, or disappeared, and which dependencies were consequently affected.

### 11. Platforms and infrastructures increasingly converge

Plantin et al. (2018) compare infrastructure studies with platform studies. Infrastructures traditionally emphasise durability, ubiquity, standards, and embedded public or collective arrangements. Platforms emphasise programmability, modularity, market intermediation, and centralised control. Large digital platforms increasingly acquire infrastructural reach, while infrastructures adopt platform-like programmability.

Cloud ERP can display a related tension. It may become deeply infrastructural to a customer's operations while remaining part of a vendor-controlled product and service platform. The provider governs releases and extension boundaries; the customer configures organisational structures, roles, workflows, and integrations; partners and adjacent services contribute further components. Analysis should identify these layers of control rather than assigning governance to one undifferentiated “system”.

## Part III: Complete technical expansion

### 12. Infrastructure properties and evidence requirements

| Property | Analytical question | Suitable evidence | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Embeddedness | Which recurrent practices depend on the arrangement? | Observation, interviews, operating records, dependency maps | Documentation shows intended relation, not embedded use |
| Reach or scope | Across which sites, groups, systems, and time periods does it operate? | Architecture records, interface inventories, longitudinal evidence | Product availability is not organisational reach |
| Installed base | What inherited systems, data, practices, skills, and institutions condition change? | Landscape analysis, migration records, historical interviews | A current-state diagram may hide the path that produced it |
| Standards | Which formats, interfaces, rules, and categories enable equivalence or connection? | Specifications, schemas, configuration and governance records | Stated standard does not prove consistent enactment |
| Visibility on breakdown | What becomes visible when the arrangement fails or conflicts? | Incident, exception, support, and recovery evidence | Absence of logged failure does not prove invisibility or reliability |
| Generativity | Who can create new functions or combinations, using which resources? | APIs, extension records, ecosystem evidence, contribution histories | Available extension points do not prove innovation |
| Control points | Who can admit, block, change, monitor, or withdraw participation? | Governance, release, access, interface and contractual evidence | Technical ownership is not the whole legitimacy question |

### 13. Classification analysis for enterprise software

For every consequential category, record:

1. the classified object;
2. the available values and hierarchy;
3. the rule or actor assigning the value;
4. the process steps, permissions, reports, or calculations that depend on it;
5. the residual, unknown, or exception category;
6. the mechanism for correction or reclassification;
7. the organisational level and release at which the category applies; and
8. the evidence required to assess practical consequences.

This turns classification from a static data field into a traceable organisational relation. It also reveals where one category coordinates several processes and where local ambiguity is deferred to later correction work.

### 14. Standards, configuration, and extension are different

| Mechanism | What it does | Main analytical risk |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | Establishes a common rule, representation, interface, or expectation | Assuming identical local meaning or performance |
| Configuration | Selects among sanctioned options for a bounded implementation | Treating selection as enactment |
| Extension | Adds or modifies behaviour through released mechanisms | Treating technical possibility as organisational adaptability |
| Integration | Connects systems or services through interfaces and mappings | Ignoring semantic reconciliation and exception work |
| Workaround | Achieves an objective outside or around the prescribed arrangement | Treating all deviation as failure or all workaround as resistance |

### 15. Cross-stream connections

**Organisation design:** Infrastructure extends differentiation and integration across systems, organisations, and time. It adds historical accumulation and installed-base dependency.

**Coordination theory:** Standards and shared classifications can coordinate dependencies, but infrastructural analysis explains how the mechanisms become embedded, repaired, and extended.

**Decision theory:** Categories and defaults distribute decision premises. Infrastructure analysis adds the historical and cross-system conditions that stabilise them.

**Enterprise systems:** Stream Four studies package architecture, implementation, and transformation. Stream Seven explains when a configured system becomes part of a wider installed base and how its evolution becomes path dependent.

**Routines and sociomateriality:** Infrastructure is reproduced through practice. Documentary evidence can identify candidate components, but not infrastructural embeddedness or enactment.

**Governance:** Standards and interfaces create control points, but technical control does not settle legitimate authority or accountability.

**Business-process management:** Process models expose only part of the infrastructure. Classifications, master data, interfaces, release rules, and cross-process records may be equally consequential.

### 16. Prohibited inferences

- A large system is not automatically infrastructure.
- A globally available product is not evidence of local infrastructural embeddedness.
- A standard process does not prove standardised practice.
- A common category is not neutral simply because it is technically necessary.
- Interoperability at interface level does not prove semantic agreement.
- An installed base is not merely legacy technology.
- An extension point does not prove generativity or useful innovation.
- Generativity does not imply absence of central control.
- Path dependence does not mean technological inevitability.
- A breakdown log does not capture all invisible maintenance and repair work.
- Reference architecture does not establish infrastructure-in-use.

### 17. Strongest defensible contribution

Stream Seven explains why enterprise software can be studied as more than a collection of applications while preventing premature claims that a reference package is already infrastructure. It directs analysis toward the standards, classifications, master data, interfaces, installed-base dependencies, control points, and release histories through which distributed work becomes connectable.

For the documentary study, the strongest defensible claim concerns represented infrastructural potential and cross-process dependency. Later implementation and field research must establish whether these arrangements become embedded, how local actors maintain them, where classification fails, and how change propagates through the installed base.

## Part IV: Audit conclusion

The verified literature supports six conclusions.

1. Infrastructure is relational and becomes visible through its place in practice, not through size alone.
2. Standards enable distributed coordination while requiring local work and producing variable consequences.
3. Classifications organise visibility and action and should be analysed as consequential arrangements.
4. Installed bases make design historical, interdependent, and path dependent.
5. Digital infrastructures may be generative while retaining significant control points.
6. A bounded ERP reference architecture can reveal candidate infrastructural components, but only implementation and practice evidence can establish infrastructure-in-use.
