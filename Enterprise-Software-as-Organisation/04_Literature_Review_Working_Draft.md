# Literature Review Working Draft

## Reading and writing approach

This draft will be developed as a cumulative explanation rather than a sequence of compressed theory summaries. Each section will introduce the organisational problem in plain language, define the relevant concepts, explain how the authors connect, apply the concepts to the research question, and state the evidential boundary. The complete theory, qualifications, and citation coverage will be preserved.

The governing requirements are recorded in the [Scholarly Writing and Teaching Standard](literature/WRITING_STANDARD.md). In the consolidated revision, every theoretical stream should enter because it solves a problem or limitation left by the preceding stream. The result should be suitable for scholarly review, doctoral learning, and later teaching without turning illustrations into findings.

## Enterprise systems and organisational transformation

Enterprise-systems scholarship has long recognised that integrated packages carry organisational consequences. Davenport (1998) argued that enterprise systems impose their own logic on strategy, organisation, and culture even as they promise information integration. Subsequent research displaced simple implementation narratives with lifecycle models, multidimensional fit accounts, and studies of adaptation. Markus and Tanis (2000) conceptualised enterprise-system experience across chartering, project, shakedown, and onward-and-upward phases. Soh, Kien and Tay-Yap (2000) showed that misalignments between package assumptions and organisational requirements are consequential and culturally situated. Hong and Kim (2002) connected organisational fit, adaptation, resistance, and implementation success in a survey of 34 organisations. Strong and Volkoff (2010) later distinguished functionality, data, usability, role, control, and organisational-culture misfits, together with deficiencies and unwanted impositions. Robey, Ross, and Boudreau (2002) placed learning and knowledge barriers at the centre of implementation, while Boudreau and Robey (2005) demonstrated that users may avoid, work around, and later reinvent enterprise technologies in emergent ways.

This literature establishes three premises for the present study. First, enterprise systems are not neutral tools: their integrated structures and process models matter organisationally. Second, reference architecture defines a structured field of implementation possibilities rather than one inevitable organisation. Third, realised effects cannot be read directly from technical design because implementation and use are contingent accomplishments. Volkoff, Strong, and Elmes (2005) further show that integration must be disaggregated into particular process, data, and organisational relations. Singh and Pekkola's (2021) systematic review distinguishes configuration, extension, and modification while showing that customisation research remains fragmented. The literature has concentrated, understandably, on selection, implementation, transformation, and use. As a result, the package's architecture is commonly treated as a source of constraints or possibilities entering those processes rather than as a primary empirical object from which an encoded model of organisation might be reconstructed.

Outcome claims require an additional boundary. Shang and Seddon (2002) classify operational, managerial, strategic, infrastructural, and organisational benefits, but their framework does not permit benefits to be read from system presence. Reference artefacts can support claims about encoded possibilities and proposed mechanisms. Configured arrangements, situated use, and outcomes require their own evidence.

Current official SAP material describes Fit-to-Standard as an Explore-phase review of preconfigured processes in a starter system, followed by documented requirements and configuration decisions. This establishes the vendor's current implementation method, not its enactment or effectiveness. The defensible research question is therefore how reference content structures implementation discussion and available choice, not whether Fit-to-Standard necessarily eliminates customisation, accelerates delivery, or produces transformation.

Brehm, Heinzl, and Markus (2001) show that implementation choice spans a tailoring spectrum rather than a binary choice between configuration and code modification. Pollock, Procter, and Williams (2003) move upstream to the vendor and trace how an ERP module is repeatedly translated between generic and specific organisational settings. Kallinikos (2004) explains why the resulting information package is organisationally consequential: it renders extensive operations and roles in procedural and transactional form. Together, these studies show that architecture deserves direct analysis, but also that it is historically produced rather than context-free.

The later cloud literature permits a limited extension. Peng and Gala (2014) identify potential benefits and barriers through interviews with 16 ERP and cloud consultants. Bjelland and Haddara (2018) examine update processes across 10 Norwegian client organisations and one cloud ERP vendor. These studies support attention to vendor-controlled and recurrent updating, but they do not establish the effects of SAP Fit-to-Standard or S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition specifically.

The proposed shift is therefore one of analytical direction. Instead of beginning with an organisation and asking how technology changes it, this study begins with a release-controlled reference architecture and asks what organisational assumptions it makes explicit and which variation mechanisms it exposes. This does not restore technological determinism or claim that enterprise-systems research ignored embedded assumptions. It isolates a prior analytical layer, encoded organisational logic, and develops a reproducible method for distinguishing it from implemented configuration, enacted practice, and organisational outcomes.

## Organisation design and interdependence

Organisation design theory supplies a vocabulary for reconstructing what enterprise architectures organise. Thompson (1967) treats organisations as open systems confronting uncertainty while seeking rationality through differentiated technical cores and coordinating arrangements. His distinction between pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence is particularly useful. Pooled interdependence joins units through their contribution to a whole and reliance on common resources; sequential interdependence introduces directional dependence; reciprocal interdependence involves mutual exchange and adjustment.

These categories make it possible to analyse process integration more precisely than by invoking integration as a general property. A reference process can represent an upstream document as a prerequisite for a downstream action, creating an explicit sequential relation. Multiple transactions can draw upon common master data or organisational structures, suggesting pooled dependence on shared classifications. Exception cycles may indicate reciprocal adjustment, although a loop in a diagram is not sufficient evidence: the analyst must identify mutually contingent action rather than simple rework.

Thompson also links forms of interdependence to coordination approaches, but software architectures complicate the relation. Machine-enforced sequencing can combine planning, rules, and status-based gating. Common data structures may coordinate without a visible managerial actor. The theory is therefore used for pattern matching, not for assuming that every observed dependency has a predetermined mechanism.

## Coordination mechanisms

Mintzberg (1979) identifies mutual adjustment, direct supervision, and standardisation of work processes, outputs, and skills as basic coordinating mechanisms. Enterprise reference architectures make several of these candidates observable. A prescribed process step and validation may standardise work; a threshold and target state may standardise an output; a routed approval may encode supervisory authority; and an exception-handling workflow may create a channel for adjustment. Standardisation of skills is less directly observable in transactional architecture and should not be inferred from a role name. It requires evidence that qualification or expertise, rather than permission alone, coordinates performance.

Coordination theory broadens the analytical focus from organisational forms to dependencies and their management. Malone and Crowston (1994) define coordination around managing dependencies among activities. This formulation fits an architectural study because it directs the analyst to identify both a dependency and a mechanism rather than code isolated functionality. Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) synthesise integrating conditions such as accountability, predictability, and common understanding. Their account helps interpret how schedules, visibility, roles, and common representations may enable collective performance, while reminding the analyst that an artefact's presence does not prove the social condition has been achieved.

The project therefore distinguishes a dependency from its proposed coordinating response. A handoff may expose a sequential relation; a status, workflow item, and shared object may jointly manage it. This configuration is the relevant unit. Treating “workflow” as coordination without specifying the dependency would produce a feature inventory rather than organisational analysis.

## Organisations as information-processing systems

Galbraith (1973, 1977) conceptualises organisation design as a response to information-processing requirements generated by uncertainty. Organisations can reduce the need for information or increase their capacity to process it through vertical information systems and lateral relations. Enterprise systems appear, at first, to be paradigmatic vertical information systems. Yet their architecture can also distribute information laterally, create shared representations, aggregate events for oversight, and signal exceptions to specialised actors.

This perspective clarifies why master data, statuses, analytical views, and workflow notifications require separate attention. They do not merely accompany work; they constitute capacities for creating, transmitting, combining, restricting, and interpreting information. A standard field can reduce equivocality by enforcing a category, but can also erase local meaning. A dashboard can increase aggregate visibility while access restrictions narrow who can see transaction detail. An exception signal reduces the search burden for attention but may depend upon thresholds that suppress weak signals.

Information-processing analysis must therefore avoid equating data integration with organisational knowledge. The study codes specific operations and asks what uncertainty they are designed to address, for which actor, and at which stage. Evidence that information is available is not evidence that it is noticed, understood, or acted upon. Those are questions for implementation and enactment research.

## Decision premises, governance, and control

Simon and March and Simon locate organisational rationality in the division of decision work and the premises that channel boundedly rational actors. Enterprise architecture can encode such premises in required data, decision tables, approval conditions, role restrictions, and default process paths. This suggests that software may distribute not only tasks but also legitimate choice. An approver receives a delimited object, information set, and set of actions; a business role defines a domain of permissible operation; an organisational restriction connects authority to a structural scope.

Governance and control should nevertheless be distinguished. Decision rights concern who may select among alternatives; access rights concern which actions or information are permitted; accountability concerns answerability and attribution. Control mechanisms specify standards, monitoring, comparisons, and consequences. A validation may control an entry without allocating approval authority. A role may confer access without demonstrating accountability. The empirical codebook retains these distinctions and permits their configuration to become a finding.

## From implemented technology to encoded organisation

Technology-and-organisation scholarship warns against treating structures embedded in artefacts as automatic social effects. Orlikowski's structurational and practice-oriented work shows that technology is constituted and reconstituted in use. Leonardi's account of materiality and human agency similarly emphasises relational affordances and constraints. Feldman and Pentland's distinction between ostensive and performative aspects of routines shows why a process representation cannot stand in for actual performances.

These insights establish the boundary of the present design. Encoded organisational logic is neither an organisation's formal structure in full nor the ostensive aspect of an enacted routine. It is a vendor-authored and technically delimited representation of legitimate actors, actions, objects, sequences, and variations. It becomes organisationally consequential only through selection, configuration, integration, and practice. Studying it remains worthwhile for the same reason that formal rules, classifications, and architectural plans are worth studying: they articulate a model of order and provide resources and constraints for subsequent action.

Infrastructure studies deepen this point. Star and Ruhleder (1996) treat infrastructure as relational and embedded in practice; Bowker and Star (1999) show that classification systems have organisational and moral consequences; later digital-infrastructure work emphasises installed bases and generativity. SAP reference architecture is not equated with infrastructure, but its organisational units, master-data categories, interfaces, and reference processes may become infrastructural when embedded across organisations. Paper 1 examines the prior encoding, not infrastructural emergence.

## Research gap and analytical response

Three bodies of knowledge meet without fully resolving the research problem. Enterprise-systems research establishes that package assumptions and standardisation matter, but typically observes them through implementation and use. Organisation-design theory explains differentiation, interdependence, coordination, information processing, and authority, but rarely treats software reference architecture as the object being designed. Practice and sociomaterial approaches correctly insist that artefacts do not determine action, but their focus can leave the vendor's prior formalisation underanalysed.

The gap is a theory-informed, empirically auditable way to reconstruct organisational assumptions from enterprise-software reference artefacts. This study addresses it through documentary and architectural analysis of SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition. It identifies software constructs, determines their operation in relation to organisational activities, codes dependency and coordination, analyses information and governance, and compares configurations across P2P, O2C, and R2R.

The candidate concept of a software organisational primitive supports this movement. A primitive is not a smallest technical component. It is a recurring construct - such as a role, workflow, status, rule, or access restriction - whose operation instantiates an organisational mechanism. Organisational meaning is relational: a status matters because it gates work or signals an exception; a role matters because it connects actors, actions, objects, and structural scopes.

## Analytical framework

The framework asks six ordered questions of each evidence segment. What differentiated activity, actor, object, or unit is represented? What dependency is present? Which primitive or configuration manages that dependency? What information operation occurs? Which right, authority, accountability, or control relation is established? What discretion remains and to whom? Thompson, Mintzberg, and Galbraith guide the theoretical codes, while governance codes remain analytically distinct.

Within-process analysis reconstructs mechanism configurations. Cross-process analysis tests whether patterns recur under different coordination conditions. Negative cases include activities without a visible coordinating mechanism, exceptions handled outside the reference process, role templates that do not allocate decisions, and configurable elements whose organisational significance is weak. The resulting claims concern the reference architecture and the organisational logic it encodes. Claims about implementation, behaviour, performance, or outcomes require different evidence and are excluded.
