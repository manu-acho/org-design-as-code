# Enterprise software as codified organisation design: a consolidated literature review

## 1. The object to be explained

An enterprise system can specify what must be known before an order is released, which role receives an exception, or which earlier record a later task must use. Those are technical arrangements. They are also organisational when they shape the relationship between differentiated activities: one person supplies information, another has a decision to make, and a third can act only once the relevant state has changed. The question is not whether the software contains a workflow or a database. It is what arrangement of work those constructs make possible, require or constrain.

The argument proceeds in four moves. Enterprise-systems research explains why a vendor reference design is neither a neutral tool nor an implemented organisation. Organisation design, coordination and decision theory explain how to reconstruct the work it represents. Governance and infrastructure research distinguish several effects too easily gathered under the word integration. Finally, process management, routine dynamics and technology research explain what the available artefacts can show and what requires evidence of practice. The nine streams therefore do different jobs in one argument.

The review is an integrative synthesis of the nine mapped streams, not an exhaustive systematic search or a new appraisal of every publication in the shared bibliography. Author-date citations indicate the scholarship used for the argument. The [stream map](03_Literature_Map.md) and [shared bibliography](references/references.bib) provide the fuller source routes. The examples in this review illustrate the analytical distinctions; they do not report the SAP findings.

## 2. A package proposes an organisation before anyone implements it

Davenport (1998) argued that an enterprise package offers integration partly by bringing its own logic to business processes and data. That logic is not a fixed organisational outcome waiting to be installed. Markus and Tanis (2000) separate the decisions made when an enterprise-system project is launched from the work of implementation, stabilisation and later use. Soh, Kien and Tay-Yap (2000) show why local requirements can be difficult to reconcile with package assumptions. [Strong and Volkoff (2010)](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol34/iss4/8/) make the problem more precise: misfit may concern functionality, data, usability, roles, control or organisational culture; it may arise because something is missing or because the package imposes something unwanted. A process can cover a functional requirement and still introduce a role or control arrangement that does not fit.

The package itself also has a history. Pollock, Procter and Williams (2003) trace how a module is made generic through translation among settings rather than designed from nowhere. Kallinikos (2004) directs attention to the procedural representation of work in information packages. These accounts matter because they prevent the gap from being overstated: embedded organisational assumptions have already been studied. What remains useful is to isolate a release-controlled reference design as an object and develop a reproducible way to reconstruct its organisational relationships before asking what a customer did with it.

Brehm, Heinzl and Markus (2001) distinguish routes for tailoring a package; the choice is not simply to accept the standard or rewrite the software. Robey, Ross and Boudreau (2002) emphasise the learning and knowledge barriers of implementation, and Boudreau and Robey (2005) show why the uses that develop cannot be read off a package. Integration, in turn, should be disaggregated into particular relations rather than treated as one property (Volkoff, Strong and Elmes, 2005). The customisation literature reviewed by Singh and Pekkola (2021) is useful for distinguishing choices, but configuration, extension and modification must be matched to what a specific edition permits. Research on cloud adoption and recurrent updates (Peng and Gala, 2014; Bjelland and Haddara, 2018) does not establish how SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition customers enact Fit-to-Standard. Nor can potential operational, managerial or other benefits be inferred merely from a capability being present (Shang and Seddon, 2002).

This yields three different tests. **Architectural coherence** asks whether the represented roles, dependencies, information and rules fit together for a specified path or variant. It is not a claim that the whole package is coherent. **Implementation fit** asks whether an organisation's selected and configured arrangement addresses its requirements and constraints. **Enacted fit** asks how that arrangement works with the actual practices through which people carry out and adjust the work. These are the analytical distinctions used here, not terms attributed to a single source. They are not a guaranteed progression: a coherent reference path can be a poor fit locally, and workable practice can depend on action that the formal configuration does not describe. Organisational outcomes remain a further question. Fit-to-Standard is consequently a useful bridge to a later implementation study, not evidence that negotiation, fit or transformation has already occurred.

## 3. From technical constructs to dependencies between activities

### Differentiation creates the problem of integration

Organisation design starts from a problem that precedes the software: dividing work makes specialised contributions possible, but those contributions must be brought together. Thompson (1967) distinguishes pooled, sequential and reciprocal interdependence. Activities can contribute to a whole or rely on a shared resource without passing work directly to one another; one activity can depend on an input produced by another; or activities can condition and adjust to one another in both directions. These are different relationships, not degrees of the same integration. A common database is insufficient evidence of pooled reliance, a list in chronological order does not establish sequential dependence, and sending work back once does not establish reciprocal adjustment.

Mintzberg (1979) addresses a different question: by what means is work coordinated? Mutual adjustment, direct supervision and standardisation of work, outputs or skills are not synonyms for the types of dependence. Nor should their correspondence be assumed. A release rule may standardise an input or procedure without identifying a supervisor, while an exception channel may make adjustment possible without demonstrating that anyone used it. Galbraith (1973, 1977) adds the information problem: uncertainty changes what must be known, and organisations may reduce the information they need or increase their capacity to process it. A shared representation can supply data across a handoff or make detail available for review. Availability, attention and understanding are three different things.

Later organisation-design research develops rather than abandons the concern with fit. Burton and Obel (2018) foreground relationships among design choices; Ethiraj and Levinthal (2004) show why decomposition and modularity must be treated as problems to be analysed, not universal prescriptions. In enterprise software, an apparently discrete module may still depend on records, classifications or permissions managed elsewhere. Conversely, co-location on the same platform does not establish integration. The analytical task is to identify which arrangements are complementary for a stated dependency and which remain separate or in tension.

### A coordinating device is not a coordination outcome

[Malone and Crowston (1994)](https://crowston.syr.edu/sites/default/files/acmcs94.pdf) make dependencies the starting point for studying coordination. Their move makes an architectural claim testable: identify the producing and consuming activities, name what passes between them, and show what the receiving activity does with it. For example, a document reference might transfer data into a later task, identify the object to be corrected, or simply be shown on screen. The same technical label does not warrant the same organisational interpretation in all three instances.

Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) distinguish mechanisms from the integrating conditions they can help create, including predictability, common understanding and accountability. A schedule can make a next action predictable in design without establishing that participants actually know when it will happen. Carlile (2002, 2004) shows why a common representation need not overcome differences in meaning or practical interests across knowledge boundaries. This connects coordination with Galbraith's information-processing perspective without equating the two: the relevant question is not simply whether a system exposes data, but what the next activity can do with the data and what remains for participants to interpret.

The resulting test is relational. Evidence of an operative input supports a stronger architectural claim than a document link shown only for navigation. A condition that changes the routing of an exception is stronger evidence of task allocation than an inbox screenshot. Neither is evidence that work was successfully coordinated. The distinction gives the analysis something more precise to compare than the presence of software features.

## 4. How rules distribute decisions without settling authority

### Decision premises and programmed action

Bounded rationality explains why organisational design is partly the design of a decision environment. Simon (1947/1997) and March and Simon (1958/1993) direct attention to the premises and programmes through which organisations structure what actors consider and how recurrent choices are handled. A **decision premise** is a fact, criterion or constraint that helps shape a decision. A required amount or category may be used to route a request; a rule may prescribe a result; an approval may leave a represented choice to a recipient. A premise for selecting a recipient is not itself the recipient's decision, and a decision to approve is not necessarily the same operation as posting or releasing the object.

This distinction also makes automation more intelligible. Some actions are represented as automated execution, not delegated judgement. Others depend on prior configuration or leave an exception for human resolution. What matters is the allocation across configuration, transaction handling and review: who can set a condition, what is determined by it, and what alternatives remain in the documented path. A supported configuration option demonstrates a bounded choice within the reference design. It does not establish who exercised that choice or what discretion remained in practice.

### Permission, authority, control and accountability

Governance asks questions that dependency management cannot settle. Technical permission concerns what an actor can access or do under specified settings. Formal authority concerns who has the recognised right to decide. Real authority concerns effective control over the decision (Aghion and Tirole, 1997). A process role, a workflow recipient and a configured permission may align, but the evidence for one cannot substitute for evidence of the others. In particular, an organisational field used to select work is not, by that fact alone, an access restriction.

Ouchi (1979) and Eisenhardt (1985) connect control arrangements with conditions such as knowledge of the work and measurability of outcomes. A procedure may coordinate by specifying an order of action and may also function as a control when a standard is applied, something is checked against it, and a consequence follows. These interpretations answer different questions. The analyst should distinguish a field offered for inspection, a validation with an operative consequence, a route for intervention, and evidence that the intervention was effective.

Accountability is more demanding still. In [Bovens's (2007) relational account](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-0386.2007.00378.x), an actor owes an account to a forum that can question and judge, with possible consequences. A record of a decision can support that relationship, but it is not the relationship itself. Likewise, a formal route to return work with a reason can be identified without claiming that people negotiated, accepted responsibility or exercised judgement as the design anticipated.

## 5. Classifications make distributed work connectable

Earlier sections explain how a record or rule connects two activities. Infrastructure research asks what makes that connection repeatable across settings and over time. Bowker and Star (1999) show why classifications are consequential: categories make some differences available for shared work and may obscure others. Edwards et al. (2009) and Timmermans and Epstein (2010) draw attention to the ongoing work through which standards are made to function. For an enterprise package, this directs attention to the use of master data, organisational categories and shared identifiers, not just to whether they exist. One field may scope a task, another may support reporting, and a third may participate in access control. Identical-looking labels are not evidence of identical functions.

Star and Ruhleder (1996) treat infrastructure as relational and embedded in practice. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) connect infrastructure design with an evolving installed base, while Tilson, Lyytinen and Sørensen (2010) foreground change and generativity in digital infrastructure. A released reference architecture is a narrower object: it can offer common classifications, interfaces and constraints that may become infrastructural, but embeddedness and the work of maintaining those connections are not visible merely because the package is broad or standardised. Release control matters methodologically for the same reason. It helps identify which arrangement the evidence describes, not how a change propagated through customers' systems.

## 6. What a process artefact can show, and where practice begins

### Process architecture is not a process trace

Business-process management supplies the vocabulary for inspecting evidence before making organisational claims. Curtis, Kellner and Over (1992) and Dumas et al. (2018) connect process representation with its purpose and perspective. Dijkman, Vanderfeesten and Reijers (2016) distinguish process architecture from a single model by focusing on how processes are organised in relation to one another. The form of the evidence determines the inference: an overview may indicate a boundary, a procedure may specify an expected transition, a configuration artefact may expose a sanctioned variant, and a successor reference may identify an interface without showing that the same transaction crossed it.

A process model is selective, even when it is useful or executable. An event log provides a different kind of evidence, but it too is a record of what the recording arrangement captured, not a transparent view of all work (van der Aalst, 2013). An exception or alternative route is therefore not peripheral. It is often the clearest way to test whether a dependency or control belongs to one branch, is configurable, or is documented as necessary for a wider scope.

### A routine artefact is not the routine

[Feldman and Pentland (2003)](https://socialecology.uci.edu/sites/socialecology.uci.edu/files/users/feldmanm/Feldman_and_Pentland_2003.pdf) distinguish the ostensive aspect, the pattern participants understand and invoke, from the performative aspect, the situated actions through which a routine is enacted. These aspects inform one another and help explain why routines can be stable and variable. A documented procedure is not automatically the ostensive aspect either: it can be a resource for how participants understand and explain a routine without showing the understanding they actually invoke. Pentland and Feldman (2008) and D'Adderio (2011) place artefacts within this relationship without reducing the routine to them. A return route in a reference process can be analysed as a formal arrangement; the pattern of follow-through, adjustment or workaround would need separate evidence.

### Material arrangements matter without determining action

Orlikowski (1992, 2000) treats technology as implicated in action and directs attention to structures enacted through use. Orlikowski and Scott (2008) take a stronger sociomaterial position that challenges separating the social and material as independent elements. Leonardi (2011) and Volkoff and Strong (2013) offer different relational accounts through which material properties, actor goals and conditions can be distinguished without predicting a uniform effect. These positions are not interchangeable. A documentary study can bracket enactment as a methodological choice without claiming that technology and practice are separable in every theoretical sense.

An **affordance** is not another word for a feature. It concerns a possibility for action in relation to a particular actor and conditions. The design can support a conditional proposition about what an actor with the relevant access and configuration could do. Whether actors perceive, actualise or benefit from that possibility is a different question. Together, routine dynamics and technology scholarship explain why neither conformity nor departure can be inferred from the mere existence of a reference procedure.

## 7. Research gap and analytical framework

The gap is one of analytical level and method, not the absence of scholarship about software and organisation. Enterprise-systems studies already explain embedded assumptions, generic package design, fit and adaptation. Organisation theory explains differentiation and the mechanisms that reconnect work. Practice research explains why that connection is not accomplished by documentation alone. The proposed contribution is a transparent way to move from a vendor artefact to an organisational interpretation while keeping represented architecture separate from customer configuration and enacted practice.

The unit of analysis is an **encoded organisational arrangement**: a bounded relation among differentiated activities, a software operation and its conditions or consequences. A **software organisational primitive** is a candidate reusable operation at a finer grain. It is not a screen name, feature count or inventory of indivisible components. A routing operation and the consequential decision nested inside it can be distinct; they do not thereby become independent evidence of recurrence. What qualifies depends on an identifiable operation, its target, its relation to work and evidence of what it does in the specified branch.

| Question for the analysis | What the evidence must establish | What is not inferred |
|---|---|---|
| What does one activity need from another? | An earlier object, data item, resource or state that the later task uses or depends on. | Dependence from chronology, reciprocity from rework or shared reliance from a common platform. |
| How is work or choice organised? | The task, rule or condition, recipient or performer, and documented branch or state consequence where relevant. | A universal approval threshold, real authority, hierarchy or exercised discretion. |
| What does information do? | Whether it supplies a later operation, makes a condition visible, or enters a check with a documented consequence. | Common understanding, accountability, effective control or action from visibility alone. |
| How far does the interpretation travel? | A stated release and scope, a contrasting route or rival, and comparison against the same evidential test in other cases. | A continuous cross-scope transaction, customer fit, routine performance, compliance or benefits. |

For Paper 1, the framework supports an architectural comparison of selected procurement, sales and financial close reference cases. It does not imply that they have equal scope or jointly trace one transaction. The findings should distinguish a claim that illustrates an established concept from a comparison that sharpens a distinction, such as identifying an object for action versus supplying data to it. The paper can then ask what the architectural account explains beyond a feature description, while leaving claims about implementation and enactment for evidence collected at those levels.
