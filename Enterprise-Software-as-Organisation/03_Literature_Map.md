# Literature Map

## Architecture of the review

The shared literature programme contains nine streams. Organisation design, coordination theory, and decision theory provide the completed explanatory foundation. Enterprise-systems research establishes the phenomenon and connects reference architecture with implementation and transformation. Routines and technology-in-practice research prevent encoded design from being mistaken for enacted work. Digital-infrastructure research adds standards, classifications, installed bases, and generativity. Governance and control clarify rights, monitoring, intervention, answerability, and consequences. Business-process management explains the representational form through which much of the empirical architecture becomes observable.

The streams are connected but not amalgamated. Each must state the problem it explains, its relationship with neighbouring streams, and the boundary beyond which another literature is required.

| No. | Stream | Foundational authors and works currently mapped | Core concepts | Development and project relevance | Central tension or gap |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Organisation design | Thompson (1967); Galbraith (1973, 1977); Mintzberg (1979) | Differentiation, integration, interdependence, uncertainty, information processing, coordination mechanisms, configuration | Explains how tasks, roles, information, decisions, and coordination arrangements form an organisational design | Developed for organisations rather than for reconstructing organisational assumptions from software architecture |
| 2 | Coordination theory | Malone and Crowston (1994); Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) | Dependencies, coordination mechanisms, accountability, predictability, common understanding | Enables fine-grained analysis of how represented mechanisms address particular dependencies | A broad definition can turn every technical relation into coordination; mechanisms do not establish outcomes |
| 3 | Decision theory | Simon (1947/1997); March and Simon (1958/1993) | Bounded rationality, decision premises, programmes, authority, discretion | Explains how categories, rules, information, defaults, and permissions distribute premises and structure choice | Formal permissions and programmes do not establish accepted authority, exercised judgement, or decision quality |
| 4 | Enterprise systems, implementation, and transformation | Davenport (1998); Markus and Tanis (2000); Soh, Kien and Tay-Yap (2000); Boudreau and Robey (2005) | Integration, process standardisation, lifecycle, misfit, adaptation, improvisation, configuration, implementation choice | Establishes the empirical phenomenon and connects encoded architecture with selection, configuration, Fit-to-Standard, local variation, and transformation | Architecture is often treated as an input to implementation rather than an organisational artefact in its own right; encoded and implemented arrangements must remain separate |
| 5 | Organisational routines | Feldman and Pentland (2003) | Ostensive and performative aspects, artefacts, recurrent patterns, stability, variation, change | Explains how documented procedures and artefacts relate to, but do not determine, recurrent performances | A reference process is neither a complete ostensive routine nor evidence of enacted performance |
| 6 | Technology, materiality, and organisation | Orlikowski (1992, 2000); Leonardi (2011) | Duality of technology, practice lens, materiality, affordance, constraint, situated use | Corrects deterministic readings and explains how technical arrangements become consequential in relation with actors and practices | Paper 1 brackets enactment and must not infer situated use from documentary architecture |
| 7 | Digital infrastructure, standards, and classification | Star and Ruhleder (1996); Bowker and Star (1999); Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010); Tilson, Lyytinen and Sørensen (2010) | Relational infrastructure, classification, standards, installed base, generativity, path dependence | Explains how shared classifications and technical arrangements organise distributed work and evolve over time | ERP reference architecture is more bounded than infrastructure, although configured systems may become infrastructural |
| 8 | Governance, control, accountability, and decision rights | Ouchi (1979); Eisenhardt (1985), with authority and decision-rights links from Stream 3 | Behaviour and output control, standards, monitoring, intervention, decision rights, answerability, consequences, legitimacy | Separates coordination and permission from governance, accountability, and control; supports analysis of approvals, monitoring, override, audit, and escalation | Existing foundations are too narrow; traceability does not establish accountability, and technical access does not establish legitimate authority |
| 9 | Business-process management and process architecture | Foundational source set to be identified and verified | Process, event, activity, actor, object, state, boundary, hand-off, variation, exception, ownership, orchestration | Provides the representational vocabulary for analysing end-to-end process artefacts before applying organisation theory | A process model is not performed work, and a standard path is not the complete organisational architecture |

## Course coverage and shared literature gaps

The postgraduate course reuses this literature architecture. It does not maintain a parallel substantive review. A new scholarly source should enter the shared bibliography, literature matrix, and relevant stream review before it is assigned in a course reading pack.

Course design revealed three priority coverage gaps. Two are now incorporated as expansions of existing streams, and one is a new ninth stream:

| Required stream or extension | Research relevance | Course modules | Current position | Required action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Business-process management and process architecture | Clarifies what process artefacts represent and how boundaries, variation, and exceptions should be interpreted | 2 and 10 | Added as Stream 9 | Conduct a bounded review before finalising Module 2 |
| Governance, control, accountability, and decision rights | Strengthens the coding and interpretation of approvals, monitoring, intervention, consequences, and answerability | 8, with links to 7 and 10 | Incorporated into the expanded scope of Stream 8 | Develop the detailed review and source assessment |
| Enterprise systems, implementation, and transformation | Connects reference architecture with selection, configuration, standardisation, adaptation, Fit-to-Standard, and implementation choices | 1 and 9 | Incorporated into the expanded scope of Stream 4 | Consolidate and audit the mapped sources, then extend only where concepts remain uncovered |

Three further areas already have foundational sources in the map but require synthesis rather than immediate expansion: routines and sociomateriality; information, attention, and visibility; and digital infrastructure, standards, and classification.

Teaching-design literature on constructive alignment, cases, assessment, accessibility, and responsible AI use will be managed separately as pedagogical infrastructure. It should not be mixed into the substantive literature review unless it bears directly on the research question.

The operational module-by-module status and non-duplication workflow are maintained in the [Course Literature Crosswalk](course/06_Literature_Crosswalk.md).

## Conceptual overlaps and disciplined separations

Standardisation of work may be both a Mintzbergian coordination mechanism and an Ouchian behaviour control. Coding may record both, but analysis must state which question each answers. A shared master-data object may coordinate pooled activity and provide information-processing capacity; it is not automatically a governance mechanism. Access restrictions govern visibility and action but do not demonstrate accountability unless consequences or answerability are documented.

The main gap is a level-of-analysis gap. Enterprise-systems studies show what happens during and after implementation; organisation-design theories explain organisational arrangements; technology-in-practice studies explain enactment. Less developed is a reproducible method for moving from vendor reference artefacts to a bounded reconstruction of encoded organisation. This project addresses that gap while using routines and sociomaterial research to police its claims.

The organisation-design stream has now been developed separately in [Stream One: Organisation Design, Detailed Literature Review](literature/Stream_01_Organisation_Design_Detailed_Review.md). Its accompanying [source assessment](literature/Stream_01_Source_Assessment.md) records which candidate sources were retained, narrowed, relocated, or excluded.

The coordination stream is developed in [Stream Two: Coordination Theory, Detailed Literature Review](literature/Stream_02_Coordination_Theory_Detailed_Review.md). Its [source assessment](literature/Stream_02_Source_Assessment.md) distinguishes full-text, abstract, metadata-only, unresolved, and rejected evidence. The stream refines the project’s coding logic by separating dependency, mechanism, artefact, integrative condition, and coordination outcome.

The decision-theory stream is developed in [Stream Three: Decision Theory, Detailed Literature Review](literature/Stream_03_Decision_Theory_Detailed_Review.md). Its [source assessment](literature/Stream_03_Decision_Theory_Source_Assessment.md) rebuilds the evidence base around Simon, March and Simon, and later extensions. It separates decision premises, programmes, authority, permissions, discretion, automation, traceability, and accountability.

The enterprise-systems stream is developed in [Stream Four: Enterprise Systems, Implementation, and Transformation](literature/Stream_04_Enterprise_Systems_Implementation_Transformation_Detailed_Review.md). Its [source assessment](literature/Stream_04_Source_Assessment.md) separates package architecture, implementation choice, configured arrangements, enacted use, and organisational outcomes. It also records the limited independent evidence currently available for public-cloud ERP and Fit-to-Standard claims.

## Priority reading route

Streams 1 to 4 provide the completed foundation. The remaining reviews should be developed in this order:

1. Stream 5, because routines establish why a documented process cannot stand for recurrent performance.
2. Stream 6, because technology, materiality, and organisation provide the main corrective to deterministic inference.
3. Stream 7, because infrastructure, standards, and classification extend the analysis across systems, organisations, and time.
4. Stream 8, because governance and control build on distinctions already stabilised in organisation design, coordination, and decision theory.
5. Stream 9, because business-process management supplies the process-representation vocabulary needed for the course, book, method, and empirical analysis.

This order is a production sequence rather than a hierarchy of theoretical importance. Sources may be collected in parallel, but each stream should receive its own detailed review and source assessment before cross-stream consolidation.

Full bibliographic records and verification flags are maintained in `references/references.bib`. The operational source sequence is maintained in `literature/Reading_List.md`, which must be updated as Streams 4 to 9 are developed.
