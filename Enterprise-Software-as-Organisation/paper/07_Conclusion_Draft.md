# Conclusion

Enterprise software can represent a division of work and provide operations that connect its parts. Across the three Swiss SAP reference cases, a record can supply downstream data or identify the object of a later action. A workflow can distinguish the premises for routing a task from the decision and its consequence. Scheduling and inspection can be separated from execution and intervention. These distinctions are visible in specific procedures and contrasts, not in a feature list or a general claim of integration.

The study asked how coordination and governance are encoded in enterprise software reference architectures. Its answer is that they are visible in the documented operations that relate activities, not in integration or workflow as general properties. A record may contribute values to a subsequent task or identify what a subsequent action is to affect. A condition may select a recipient without deciding the case. A specified action may produce an expected change in state, while a monitor may do no more than make a condition inspectable. Treating these operations as interchangeable would obscure how the reference design distributes information, decision work and responsibility for action.

The alternatives are central to that answer. In J45, an invoice route without a purchase-order reference shows that the dependency on the purchasing record belongs to the referenced route, not to every invoice. In J58, direct posting and verification before posting illustrate different arrangements leading to the same type of accounting state. In BD9, selecting delivery work and passing a confirmed quantity to billing show two ways that information can enter a later activity. None of these examples shows that the documented paths were exercised by a customer. They do show why the conditions, targets and expected consequences of an operation must remain visible when comparing cases.

The contribution is a way to reconstruct codified organisation design from such relationships. The method makes the proposed arrangement and its evidence inspectable while retaining the alternatives that narrow it. A documented reference arrangement remains different from a configured customer system and from work as performed. That difference is not a qualification added after the analysis; it is what defines the claim the evidence can sustain.

Methodologically, the comparison shows what a source-controlled documentary study can contribute before access to an implementation is available. It can recover the represented operation, state the relation it appears to organise, and ask whether a rival explanation or an alternative path changes that interpretation. It can also report when the evidence is too thin. In this study, a recurring pattern of scoped work did not support an architecture-wide claim about access, and the names of succeeding processes did not establish that one transaction was traced through settlement and close. Those exclusions make the remaining conclusions more specific: the paper identifies documented arrangements for connecting work, not a universal inventory of mechanisms or a measure of their effects.

The next empirical question is how these arrangements change when they are put to work. An implementation study could follow a selected reference path through the decision to adopt or adapt it, the resulting configuration, and tests of the relevant exception. A study of enactment could examine who supplies the information, who effectively decides, and what people do when the represented path does not fit the situation. Such studies would not simply confirm this account at a larger scale. They would examine different objects and could show that a coherent reference arrangement is changed, supplemented or bypassed for reasons the vendor documents cannot reveal.

The value of treating enterprise software as codified organisation design is therefore not to assume that the package determines the organisation. It is to make the proposed division and coordination of work concrete enough to compare, challenge and investigate. A reference design can tell us where to look and what to test. Whether that design fits an organisation, and whether it produces the intended results, remains a question for evidence of configuration and practice.
