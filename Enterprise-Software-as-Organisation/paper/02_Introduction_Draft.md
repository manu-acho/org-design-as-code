# Introduction

A document identifier can be an administrative convenience, an input to a later task, or a means of finding the record on which a correction is to be made. The screen may look much the same in each instance. The organisational consequence is not. If a later activity needs data produced by an earlier one, the reference between records helps arrange how the two activities connect. If the identifier only locates a record, a different operation is involved. This difference is a starting point for analysing enterprise software as organisation design.

Consider what happens when an invoice is entered with reference to a purchase order. In one documented SAP route, entering the reference supplies items to the invoice. The earlier purchasing record does some of the work of assembling information for Accounts Payable. In another route, the accountant enters general-ledger and other accounting data without that reference. Both routes can result in a posted invoice, but they do not connect accounting to procurement in the same way. The contrast makes a general question concrete: what does the software require one part of an organisation to provide before another part can act? A description of integrated purchasing and accounting is too coarse to answer it. Conversely, describing the difference as a choice of entry screen tells us too little about the responsibilities and information involved. The difference lies in what each route takes as its input, what remains for the accountant to enter or check, and which earlier activity is needed to supply the record.

A similar problem arises when a system offers an approval function. A condition that selects which case reaches an inbox is not the decision taken by its recipient. The recipient's decision is in turn distinct from the change in the status of the underlying record. Yet all three may be described as approval in a functional overview. To understand the arrangement, it matters whether the system is selecting work, presenting it to a role, recording a choice, or applying the consequence of that choice. An apparently minor distinction between technical operations can change the answer to an organisational question: what must happen before a later activity can begin, and what kind of decision is involved?

Enterprise-systems scholarship has long recognised that integrated packages carry organisational assumptions. They bring a logic for data and work, may conflict with local requirements, and can be adapted or used in ways that diverge from the package design (Davenport, 1998; Soh, Kien and Tay-Yap, 2000; Boudreau and Robey, 2005). The question is not whether this literature noticed embedded assumptions. It is whether a reference architecture can be examined directly, at a sufficiently precise level to say which arrangement of work it offers and where that interpretation stops.

The difference is particularly important when the object of inquiry is a reference design rather than an implementation. Research that follows an enterprise system into an organisation can examine the work of reconciling package assumptions with operating requirements, as well as how people use, resist or revise the result. Such research needs a characterisation of the arrangement that is being configured or adapted. A reference design offers one starting point, but it also contains alternatives, and a single procedure cannot stand for the entire package. The relevant baseline is therefore not an abstract standard process. It is a specified route and its documented variants, located in a particular edition, release and localisation. Establishing that baseline makes it easier to distinguish a change in the reference offer from a configuration decision or a change in local practice.

The gap addressed here is accordingly a gap in analytical resolution. Studies of package fit have already identified that the design of roles, data and controls matters. Studies of implementation and use have already explained why the resulting arrangements cannot be predicted from the package. What remains to be shown is how a researcher can move from the public description of a specific software operation to a defensible statement about the relationship between activities. This involves more than identifying a feature that appears organisationally relevant. It requires identifying its inputs, recipient or target, conditions and represented consequences, and asking whether a different branch changes the proposed interpretation.

This study asks: **How are organisational coordination and governance mechanisms encoded within enterprise software reference architectures?** It examines SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition as an instrumental case, comparing three embedded Swiss reference cases in the 2608 release family: Procurement of Direct Materials (J45), Sell from Stock (BD9), and Accounting and Financial Close (J58). These cases expose different tasks and conditions. They are not treated as exhaustive specifications of Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash and Record-to-Report, or as consecutive parts of a single observed transaction.

The comparison is deliberately within one reference environment. Procurement, sales fulfilment and financial close provide different settings in which to examine comparable questions. A procurement reference may populate an invoice. A sales reference may help select items for delivery, while a separately documented delivery quantity enters billing. In financial close, the number of an earlier clearing entry may identify the record to be reset. These cases permit comparison without treating the recurrence of a field or interface as recurrence of a mechanism. They also make differences visible: a process can rely on a particular record for one route without making it compulsory in another, and two procedures can share a state label while arriving at that state through different arrangements.

Organisation design supplies three complementary questions. Thompson (1967) asks what kind of dependence links differentiated activities. Mintzberg (1979) asks how the work is coordinated. Galbraith (1973, 1977) asks what information the work requires and what processing capacity an arrangement offers. Decision and governance research adds questions about who is expected or permitted to act, what premises shape a choice and what a decision changes. Together, these perspectives make it possible to ask what a feature does in a relation, rather than taking its technical label as an organisational explanation.

The study combines qualitative documentary analysis with architectural reconstruction. It first identifies what a source represents, then interprets an operation in relation to the tasks it connects, assigns or constrains. Exact procedures and contrasting branches are used to test whether that interpretation adds anything to a technical explanation. The paper proposes the software organisational primitive as a candidate unit for this analysis: a reusable type of operation whose organisational relevance can be shown in a specified relation. It does not treat every role, rule or screen as a primitive, or claim a validated inventory.

The paper distinguishes architectural coherence from implementation fit and enacted fit. A reference route can be examined for the way its parts fit together without establishing that it meets an organisation's requirements or works as intended in practice. The findings therefore describe encoded dependencies, conditional action and opportunities for inspection or intervention. They do not establish actual authority, coordination effectiveness or organisational performance. Fit-to-Standard motivates a later question about the relationship between reference design and implementation rather than a finding about observed design negotiation.

The paper makes three contributions within this scope. The first is to specify the object of study. A vendor reference architecture can be examined as a representation of differentiated work, including the dependencies and choices it makes available, without treating it as an organisation in operation. The second is methodological. The paper sets out a procedure for locating an exact documentary segment, describing its technical operation, interpreting its relevance to work and then testing that interpretation against alternatives and rival explanations. This makes the reasoning inspectable by a reader who might accept the source description but dispute the organisational interpretation. The third is empirical and bounded: the comparison identifies recurring distinctions between record use, routing, decision, inspection and intervention, while showing where broader propositions about access or control are not supported.

These contributions are related. If an analyst does not know which operation is being compared, a finding of recurrence across business processes may amount to little more than a list of similar application functions. If the operation is specified without its branch conditions, a finding about one procedure can silently become a general rule. If the architecture is treated as evidence of how work is performed, a formally assigned decision can become an unsupported claim about exercised authority. The analysis therefore keeps the technical explanation in view and asks what the same evidence can additionally show about the work that must be connected. A documentary claim earns its organisational interpretation by answering that question, not by replacing the technical account.

This distinction has a practical implication for what an organisation should ask of a reference design. It is possible to inspect whether a documented path links an order, receipt and invoice appropriately for the task it represents. That examination cannot resolve who should own an exception in a particular business, whether a direct route is permitted under local policy, or whether participants maintain the records on time. Those questions are not failures of architectural analysis. They identify what must be investigated next and what kinds of evidence are needed. A study of the reference design can thus narrow the questions posed to implementation and field research without pre-empting their answers.

The next section develops the analytical framework. The method then explains the case boundaries and tests used to compare procedures. The findings follow records, decisions and execution across the three cases; the discussion explains what the comparisons sharpen and what remains outside their evidential reach.
