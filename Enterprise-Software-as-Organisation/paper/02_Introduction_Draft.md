# Introduction  -  Working Draft

Enterprise software is among the most extensive formal architectures through which contemporary organisations represent and conduct work. An integrated enterprise system defines business objects, organisational units, roles, permissions, process steps, status transitions, validations, approvals, and exceptions. These constructs are normally described as functionality. Yet each also addresses an organisational problem: how work is divided, how dependent activities are connected, which information is shared or withheld, who may decide, and how action is controlled.

Information-systems research has established that enterprise systems are organisationally consequential. Integrated packages can impose process and data disciplines, expose misfits, and become objects of adaptation and improvisation (Davenport, 1998; Soh, Kien and Tay-Yap, 2000; Boudreau and Robey, 2005). This research appropriately rejects simple technological determinism. Organisations select, configure, extend, interpret, circumvent, and enact systems in different ways. Most studies therefore follow the system into implementation and use.

That emphasis leaves a prior analytical object less fully developed: the organisational model represented in the vendor's reference architecture. Before an organisation configures a package, the package already distinguishes actors and objects, makes some sequences possible, supplies standard roles and processes, and delimits sanctioned variation. These arrangements do not determine practice, but neither are they organisationally empty. They are an explicit, inspectable attempt to make collective work computable.

Organisation theory provides a precise way to interrogate this object. Thompson (1967) distinguishes pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence and links them to different coordination demands. Mintzberg (1979) differentiates direct supervision, mutual adjustment, and standardisation of work, outputs, and skills. Galbraith (1973, 1977) treats organisation design as the allocation of information-processing capacity under uncertainty. Read together - but not collapsed - these theories direct attention from software features to relations among differentiated work, coordinating mechanisms, and information requirements. Decision and control perspectives further clarify how rules and permissions distribute legitimate choice.

The puzzle is that software architecture is simultaneously technical and organisational. A workflow can be read as executable routing and as an allocation of tasks and authority. A master-data object can be read as a data model and as a common classification coordinating dispersed activities. A status can preserve transaction consistency and signal when another actor may proceed. The organisational interpretation is plausible only when it is reconstructed through explicit evidence and when technical and documentary rivals are considered.

This study asks: **How are organisational coordination and governance mechanisms encoded within enterprise software reference architectures?** It examines SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition as an instrumental case, focusing on Procure-to-Pay, Order-to-Cash, and Record-to-Report. These domains provide variation in supplier/material flows, customer fulfilment, and financial integration. Methodologically, the study combines qualitative documentary analysis with architectural reconstruction. Descriptive coding identifies actors, actions, objects, handoffs, rules, statuses, rights, and exceptions. Theoretical coding then analyses interdependence, coordination, information processing, governance, and discretion. Within-process mechanism maps are compared across domains; negative cases and rival interpretations discipline inference. The unit is not an isolated feature but an encoded organisational arrangement linking differentiated activities to an operative software construct.

The paper develops the candidate concept of a *software organisational primitive*: a recurring software construct through which an organisational coordination, governance, information-processing, or control mechanism is instantiated. Roles, workflows, rules, statuses, and access rights are candidates, not presumed members. A construct qualifies only when its organisational operation can be evidenced. Its meaning is relational: the same status may coordinate sequence, signal exception, or merely display state.

The intended contribution is threefold, subject to empirical support. First, the paper establishes encoded organisational logic as an analytical level distinct from implemented organisational configuration and enacted organisational practice. Second, it offers a mechanism-centred method for moving from reference artefact to organisational inference. Third, it assesses whether classical organisation-design constructs require refinement when coordination and governance are operationalised through software primitives.

The boundary is central. Documentary evidence cannot show that customers implement the reference arrangement, that users enact it as designed, or that coordination improves. The paper therefore does not ask how SAP improves organisation. It asks what organisational assumptions are embedded in the architecture and how organisation theory can reconstruct them. Fit-to-Standard is treated as a sensitising bridge to later research, not as an observed negotiation in this study.
