# Discussion

## What an architectural analysis explains

The comparison supports treating reference architecture as an inspectable proposal for organising work. Its value is not that it reveals a complete organisation. It shows where a later task has something specific to use, where a condition assigns a task, and where an operation changes state or only makes it visible. Technical and organisational accounts can coexist: an identifier may support transaction integrity and at the same time organise a dependency between activities. The organisational account adds value only if it explains a consequence for differentiated work.

The record comparison sharpens the use of sequential interdependence in architectural analysis. The cases illustrate Thompson's distinction between temporal order and an operative dependence when interpreted alongside Malone and Crowston's dependency-centred approach. They also suggest a practical refinement for documentary analysis: test whether a reference **supplies data** or **identifies the object of action**. Both connect work, but they imply different information and control requirements. This is a refinement of how the analytical categories are applied to reference architecture, not a revision or validation of the general theory of interdependence.

The workflow comparison provides a similar distinction for decision theory. Conditions that select a route, a recipient's represented decision and the consequence for the object should be analysed separately. An approval label or inbox alone cannot distinguish these operations. For coordination theory, a formal return makes a possible feedback route visible, but it does not establish mutual adjustment. For information-processing theory, visibility creates represented capacity; a quantity used by billing or a matching condition that gates clearing gives more specific evidence of how information enters an operation. None of these comparisons shows whether participants perceived the information as useful or whether an organisation's information-processing demands were met.

### What changes when the software operation becomes the unit of comparison

The main theoretical gain is a way to connect work design and software design without making them equivalent. Thompson's (1967) account starts with the dependencies created by differentiated work. The documentary analysis starts at the other end, with a concrete operation, and asks which of those dependencies it represents. When a prior purchase order supplies items to invoicing, the relation is not simply that Purchasing goes first and Accounts Payable follows. The later task uses information produced in earlier work. In a clearing reset, the prior record instead determines the target of the corrective action. This distinction is useful because a proposed change to the two arrangements would raise different questions. For data provision, the concern is whether the next task receives the relevant and sufficiently reliable values. For target identification, the concern is whether the action is directed at the correct existing object. A common record feature can thus support different designs of the handoff.

The alternative invoicing route also shows why a mechanism should not be identified solely by the result it supports. A comparable accounting result can be represented with different inputs and different dependencies. Standardisation, in Mintzberg's (1979) sense, does not imply that there is only one prescribed path. Standardised alternatives can place different demands on the people and records involved. A locally appropriate choice could depend on what upstream evidence exists, who is responsible for supplying accounting data and what risks the organisation needs to manage. Those are implementation questions. The documentary contribution is to make the choice legible by distinguishing the offered arrangements before judging them. This is consistent with enterprise-system fit research, which shows that requirements can concern roles and controls as well as functionality (Strong and Volkoff, 2010). It explains why matching feature names to requirements is not enough to identify what would have to fit.

The findings also suggest how to use information-processing theory more carefully in an architectural study. A system can change what is available to a role, what is carried into the next task, or what determines whether an operation proceeds. These are different ways of structuring an information environment. Galbraith's (1973, 1977) account helps explain why they matter when work is uncertain or distributed. It does not tell us that more visibility is always better or that a data transfer resolves a coordination problem. An overdue item may become visible without an intervention being specified. A confirmed quantity may enter billing without establishing a shared interpretation of a delivery discrepancy. In Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) terms, mechanisms and the integrating conditions they might help create should remain distinct. The paper makes that separation concrete by identifying the operation for which the document provides evidence before drawing an inference about its relevance.

### What can be said about authority and control

The strongest governance result is a distinction, not a map of who governs the enterprise. Decision premises, routing, a represented choice, system permissions and consequences for a record are analytically separable. This extends Simon's focus on the premises of decisions to a concrete architectural question: which premises are used to make a task available, and which decisions remain to be made after it is available? It also explains why a result such as posting should not be assigned to the person who prepared or submitted the entry merely because that person initiated the process. Figure 5 makes the distinction clear, but the same reasoning applies whenever two paths can reach the same type of state through different allocations of work.

A stronger access finding would require evidence that the relevant restrictions attach to the actions and actors under comparison. The procedure-specific company-code check in J58 is informative precisely because it does more than name a role or field. It does not establish that similar fields in sales and procurement perform an equivalent access function. Nor does the presence of a formal workflow indicate that real authority follows the represented path (Aghion and Tirole, 1997). An analyst could test the configured relation with permission assignments and representative scenarios. Establishing real authority would still require evidence about how decisions are made and influenced, including cases where the system's named recipient is not the effective decision maker. The architectural contribution is to specify which relationship requires that follow-on test.

A comparable restraint applies to accountability. Trace records and review screens can make an account easier to reconstruct. They do not establish an obligation to give an account, a forum that can question it, or consequences when the account is inadequate (Bovens, 2007). Similarly, a specified matching condition is evidence of a represented control, but it does not show whether a relevant problem is detected, whether exceptions are resolved appropriately, or whether similar cases are treated consistently. These are not semantic differences. They affect the choice of evidence. Logs can show what was recorded; review records can show who responded; interviews and observation can illuminate how participants understood the obligation and dealt with exceptions. An architectural study can distinguish those research questions before choosing how to answer them.

## Primitives and the limits of reuse

The candidate primitive concept is useful when it keeps attention on a reusable operation rather than a catalogue of product features. Record reference, conditional routing, scheduling and inspection are useful distinctions because apparently similar forms do different work under different conditions. A routing rule and its nested approval are not independent instances of one mechanism. Likewise, visibility and intervention should not be counted as the same control. [Table 6](../figures/Table_06_Candidate_Operations.md) records these distinctions as candidates. The study does not establish an exhaustive taxonomy, a stable set of primitives for all systems, or a causal claim about their combined effects.

The evidence for task scoping is stronger than the evidence for an architecture-wide allocation of access. A field can delimit the set of work selected for an operation, while permissions are configured elsewhere. The J58 forwarding check supports a specific procedure-level claim; it does not support replication of effective permissions across procurement and sales. This is a substantive limit on the governance argument. The analysis can reconstruct represented routing and consequences without treating them as actual decision rights, supervision or separation of duties.

Reusability has two implications. First, comparisons should hold the grain of the operation steady. Routing should be compared with routing, not with every action that happens after a routed task reaches its recipient. Second, the category must leave room for the context that gives the operation its meaning. A scheduled purchase-order conversion and a scheduled ledger job both separate aspects of timing from execution. That is enough to compare a bounded organisational operation. It is not enough to equate the business decisions, the data required, or the consequences of errors. Where that distinction cannot be preserved, the category is too broad to support a useful explanation. The candidate vocabulary is valuable as a means of generating comparable questions, not as a way to erase the differences among domains.

The comparison also suggests a disciplined way to evaluate a proposed primitive. Identify the concrete operation and its object, show how it connects or constrains differentiated work, and look for a nearby route that changes its input or consequence. Then ask whether the candidate travels to another case without depending on the original product label. If it travels only at the level of the technical facility, describe it at that level. If an alternative displaces its organisational function, qualify the claim to the relevant route. For example, it is reasonable to compare whether jobs provide a represented opportunity for inspection. It would be unreasonable to turn those opportunities into a count of completed reviews. This approach also keeps uncertainty visible: an operation can be promising as a comparison category while its frequency, independence and wider applicability remain unknown.

A second implication is that architectural coherence is not the accumulation of individually plausible operations. It concerns whether the operations fit together for the work under study. A record can provide usable input to a task while the route for an exception remains unspecified. A process can have a represented recipient and still leave unresolved which access conditions would apply after configuration. In either situation, the analysis should indicate where the evidence is coherent for a specified link and where it does not warrant an overall judgement. This matters especially in integrated packages. Integration across records may be well described at one point and only signalled by a successor reference at another. A claim about the whole flow cannot be assembled by giving the weaker links the evidential strength of the strongest one.

### Use the reference design to ask better implementation questions

The approach has a practical use before a configuration decision is made. Rather than asking whether a package supports invoice verification, a team can ask what information the proposed route takes from procurement, which checks it applies, who resolves an exception and what a changed state permits. Rather than asking whether approval exists, it can distinguish the criteria used to route the task from the decision itself and from any separate technical permission. The comparison of reference paths can reveal a requirement that would otherwise be treated as a feature request. The issue might not be the absence of a screen, for example, but the need to maintain a traceable exception when an upstream reference is unavailable. These are diagnostic questions suggested by the analysis. The study does not assess their use in a workshop or claim that answering them improves implementation.

Fit-to-Standard provides a setting in which those questions could be examined. A future study could compare the offered reference paths with what workshop participants select, which requirements justify an exception, and how the decision is translated into configuration or an extension. An especially informative case would be one where participants agree that a standard feature is available but disagree about the responsibility or decision it should represent. Following the decision record into configuration and testing would show whether the stated arrangement became the implemented one. Observing what happens after go-live would address a different issue: whether people follow the arrangement, supplement it with other coordination, or bypass it under particular conditions. The reference reconstruction can provide a baseline for these comparisons without being mistaken for any of the later evidence.

The same logic suggests what to ask when changing an existing design. If a team proposes to automate an operation, it should identify which task is being automated and what happens to its upstream inputs, downstream reliance and exception handling. Moving the timing of a job, routing a request to a different role and replacing a discretionary decision with a programmed rule are not equivalent changes. A credible evaluation would have to trace the change at the appropriate level and compare normal and exceptional cases. The paper's method can specify the comparison; only customer-specific evidence could establish its effects.

## Limitations and follow-on research

The corpus is selective, vendor-authored and scoped to one product edition, release family and localisation. The case set supports analytical comparison within that boundary rather than statistical inference or cross-platform generalisation. Test procedures may leave implicit how a particular configuration resolves competing rules or what happens after a documented branch. Overviews and successor links establish useful context but not same-instance execution. The comparison and targeted recheck improve traceability; they are not independent recoding, tenant verification or observational evidence.

The limitations are productive for research design. First, vendor documentation is more likely to show what is offered or expected than how it performs under an organisation's conditions. It can represent exceptions, but the documented exceptions are not a sample of those that actually occur. Second, differences in detail across scope items can create apparent differences in architecture. If one script describes a review state and another does not, the appropriate result may be that the comparison cannot be made, not that review is absent. Third, release and localisation control make the cases interpretable, but do not make the results stable across versions or territories. Testing whether a relationship survives a release change would require comparing the procedures and their conditions explicitly rather than relying on continuity of a scope identifier.

The single-researcher analysis also creates a limit on how much the coding trail can establish. The decision records expose the alternatives considered and the grounds for accepting or narrowing a claim. They do not show that another analyst would make the same classifications. An independent reader could test the approach by reconstructing selected routes from the source locators before comparing interpretations. Disagreement would be informative if it identified an ambiguous boundary between, for instance, task selection and use of downstream data. A different test would use a configured environment to reproduce the documented consequences under explicitly stated conditions. Those two extensions address different uncertainties: the dependability of the analysis and the behaviour of a configured system.

A cross-case follow-up should also be designed to expose failure of the categories, not just find more illustrations. One possibility is to select a procedure in another platform whose visible workflow resembles a Swiss SAP example, then compare whether it actually uses the same kind of reference, condition or intervention. Another is to hold a business function constant and compare localisations or releases that implement it differently. Either design could reveal where the proposed vocabulary is portable and where it depends on a product-specific convention. The criterion for transfer would be an equivalent relation supported by comparable evidence, not a familiar role name or diagram.

Architectural coherence, implementation fit and enacted fit therefore remain separate. Research on implementation would need to observe which options are selected, what requirements and exceptions are negotiated, and how decisions are translated into configuration. Research on enactment would need traces, interviews or observation tied to the relevant tasks and roles. A cross-platform study could test whether the same operation-based distinctions travel beyond the SAP reference case. Such work would test the reach of the framework rather than assume it from a shared vocabulary.
