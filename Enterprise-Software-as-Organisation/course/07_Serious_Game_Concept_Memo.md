# Serious Game Concept Memo

**Working title:** Enterprise Design Lab: The Organisation Behind the System  
**Status:** Deferred concept  
**Relationship:** Companion to the postgraduate course  
**Development priority:** Resume after the nine literature streams and initial theory-led course modules are complete

## Purpose

This memo records a possible serious game derived from the research programme and postgraduate course. The game would allow students and practitioners to experience how enterprise-software design choices distribute work, information, coordination, authority, control, and discretion.

The concept is intentionally deferred. It should not distract from completing the literature streams, consolidated literature review, theory-led book chapters, and course modules.

## Core proposition

Players form an enterprise-transformation team responsible for designing and operating an end-to-end process. They must select roles, workflows, information arrangements, rules, approvals, controls, automation, exception routes, and escalation mechanisms. The game then introduces uncertainty, conflicting objectives, and process exceptions that reveal the consequences and limitations of the chosen architecture.

The game should make one central idea experiential:

> A formal architecture can allocate roles, information, permissions, workflows, and decision opportunities, but it cannot guarantee shared understanding, accepted authority, sound judgement, or coordinated performance.

## Intended audiences

The first version should support the postgraduate course. Later adaptations may serve:

- advanced undergraduate students;
- executive education participants;
- SAP and enterprise-software professionals;
- transformation leaders;
- process owners;
- enterprise architects;
- governance and control professionals;
- internal transformation academies.

## Recommended initial format

The first prototype should be a facilitated tabletop simulation. This format is inexpensive to revise, suitable for classroom and executive use, and capable of making negotiation and informal influence visible.

A later digital simulation may be considered only after the mechanics, causal assumptions, and teaching value have been tested.

## Possible player roles

- Transformation Director
- Global Process Owner
- Finance Lead
- Procurement or Operations Lead
- Enterprise Architect
- Local Business Lead
- Risk and Compliance Lead
- Data Owner
- Implementation Partner

Each role should receive legitimate objectives and constraints. The game should not make one function the obvious source of correct answers.

## Principal design choices

Players may be required to determine:

- process sequence and hand-offs;
- organisational roles and task allocation;
- information visibility and access;
- decision premises, thresholds, and defaults;
- approval and release structures;
- centralisation and local discretion;
- automation and manual judgement;
- monitoring, controls, and traceability;
- exception handling and escalation;
- configuration, extension, or organisational change.

## Event and exception possibilities

The simulation may introduce events such as:

- an invoice failing to match a goods receipt;
- an unavailable supplier or approver;
- inconsistent master data;
- a local legal requirement conflicting with a global template;
- an automated rule blocking a legitimate transaction;
- a customer order exceeding a credit threshold;
- a cross-functional exception requiring expertise;
- a local workaround weakening traceability;
- an audit finding that produces pressure for additional control;
- an AI agent recommending or executing an action.

Events should test the architecture under uncertainty rather than merely reward recall of a prescribed process.

## Research concepts as game mechanics

| Research concept | Candidate game mechanic |
|---|---|
| Pooled interdependence | Shared resource, standard, or master-data pool |
| Sequential interdependence | Required hand-off or prerequisite |
| Reciprocal interdependence | Repeated exchange and adjustment among roles |
| Coordination mechanism | Workflow, schedule, role, rule, or shared object |
| Accountability | Visible ownership combined with an answerability relation |
| Predictability | Capacity to anticipate timing, state, or another actor's contribution |
| Common understanding | Interpretation challenge not resolved automatically by shared data |
| Decision premise | Category, rule, threshold, target, or required fact |
| Programme | Standard response to a recognised situation |
| Permission | Technically available action |
| Authority | Capacity to have a decision accepted as a premise for action |
| Discretion | Legitimate range of available judgement |
| Discretion relocation | Movement of judgement to designers, configurators, data owners, or exception handlers |
| Control | Standard, monitoring, comparison, intervention, and possible consequence |
| Exception | Case that the normal programme cannot adequately resolve |
| Encoded design | Initial architecture available to players |
| Implemented configuration | Design selections made during setup |
| Enacted practice | Actions, negotiations, repairs, and workarounds during play |

## Game loop

1. Receive an organisational context and competing objectives.
2. Design the process architecture.
3. Allocate roles, information, decisions, controls, and exceptions.
4. Run normal transactions or cases.
5. Introduce uncertainty and disruption.
6. Observe delays, conflicts, failures, workarounds, and unintended consequences.
7. Revise the architecture.
8. Compare intended design with enacted play.
9. Complete a theory-led debrief.

## Scoring principle

The game should not use one universal success score. It should represent trade-offs among:

- speed;
- reliability;
- cost;
- control effectiveness;
- data consistency;
- local responsiveness;
- adaptability;
- workload;
- traceability;
- resilience;
- customer or supplier impact;
- technical and organisational complexity.

No design should be universally optimal. Outcomes should depend on context, interdependence, uncertainty, knowledge distribution, and organisational priorities.

## Debrief questions

The facilitated debrief should ask:

1. Which dependencies were present?
2. Which mechanisms were intended to manage them?
3. Which mechanisms produced useful conditions, and which failed?
4. Was a breakdown technical, organisational, semantic, political, or mixed?
5. Where was authority formally located, and who exercised effective influence?
6. Where did discretion move after automation or standardisation?
7. Which information was available but unusable or unactionable?
8. Which control created an unintended consequence?
9. What did the architecture encode?
10. What emerged only through situated play?
11. What evidence would be required to claim that the same effect occurs in organisations?

## Evidence and validation rules

- Early scenarios must be labelled hypothetical or illustrative.
- SAP-specific cases may be added only from the controlled corpus.
- Simulated relationships must not be described as empirically validated organisational effects without supporting evidence.
- Game scores are instructional models, not predictions of implementation outcomes.
- Mechanics should be reviewed against the completed literature streams.
- Any use of gameplay as research data requires a separate method, ethics review where applicable, consent process, and analytical protocol.

## Vendor and intellectual-property position

The core mechanics should remain vendor-neutral. SAP may provide a principal case or optional scenario pack after source, trademark, copyright, screenshot, and distribution conditions have been reviewed.

This structure broadens the audience, reduces release dependence, and preserves the theoretical contribution beyond a single platform.

## Development gates

### Gate 1: theoretical readiness

- all nine literature streams completed;
- relevant concepts and boundaries stabilised;
- theory-led course modules drafted;
- game learning outcomes aligned with the course.

### Gate 2: prototype readiness

- one bounded process selected;
- hypothetical tabletop scenario written;
- roles, choices, events, and debrief mapped to theory;
- causal and scoring assumptions explicitly documented;
- internal review confirms that no mechanic is presented as an empirical fact without evidence.

### Gate 3: evidence-based case readiness

- target SAP release and process scope frozen;
- relevant artefacts registered in the shared corpus;
- case provenance and permissions checked;
- illustrative details replaced or supplemented with verified documentary evidence.

### Gate 4: pilot readiness

- facilitator guide completed;
- participant instructions tested;
- accessibility review completed;
- pilot feedback and revision protocol established;
- research use, if any, separated from teaching evaluation.

## Deferred next actions

When this concept is resumed:

1. confirm the learning outcomes;
2. select P2P or another bounded initial process;
3. produce a one-page game design specification;
4. build a paper prototype;
5. test whether the mechanics express the theory accurately;
6. revise the debrief before expanding game complexity;
7. consider a digital version only after successful facilitated pilots.

## Current decision

Preserve this concept as a future companion to the course. Do not begin active game development until the literature and initial theory-led course work reach Gate 1.
