# Learning Outcomes and Constructive Alignment

## Programme learning outcomes

By the end of the postgraduate master course, a successful student should be able to:

1. Explain how enterprise-software reference architecture can represent organisational arrangements.
2. Distinguish encoded organisational logic, implemented configuration, enacted practice, and organisational outcomes.
3. Reconstruct activities, actors, objects, and process boundaries from controlled reference artefacts.
4. Identify pooled, sequential, reciprocal, shared-resource, fit, producer-consumer, and knowledge dependencies where evidence permits.
5. Relate documented dependencies to candidate coordination mechanisms without treating mechanisms as outcomes.
6. Analyse how information structures create, transmit, restrict, aggregate, or direct attention to organisational information.
7. Distinguish shared representation from shared interpretation and analyse knowledge boundaries.
8. Identify decision premises, programmes, alternatives, criteria, defaults, thresholds, and exception structures.
9. Distinguish permission, responsibility, approval opportunity, authority, discretion, accountability, and control.
10. Analyse how automation may distribute or relocate judgement rather than assuming that it removes judgement.
11. Compare organisational configurations across end-to-end processes and formulate rival interpretations.
12. Produce a theoretically grounded, traceable, and evidence-bounded analysis of an enterprise-software artefact or process.

## Performance progression

The curriculum should move through four levels of capability:

1. **Recognise:** Identify concepts and documentary traces accurately.
2. **Differentiate:** Avoid common category errors and distinguish neighbouring constructs.
3. **Analyse:** Connect evidence, mechanism, theory, and boundary in a defensible interpretation.
4. **Evaluate and design:** Compare configurations, assess alternatives, and formulate evidence needs or design questions.

Students should not be assessed on evaluation before they have demonstrated reliable recognition and differentiation.

## Minimum evidence standard for student work

A strong analytical claim should state:

- the observed artefact or operation;
- its precise evidence location;
- the organisational dependency or problem;
- the proposed theoretical interpretation;
- at least one plausible rival interpretation;
- the scope of the claim;
- what additional evidence would be required to move from encoded design to implementation or practice.
